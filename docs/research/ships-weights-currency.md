## 1. Vessels Calling at Melaka (c. 1511)

Melaka sat at the hinge of the Indian Ocean and South China Sea monsoon systems, hosting ocean-going cargo ships from Gujarat, Malabar, Coromandel, Bengal, Pegu, China, Ryukyu, and Java alongside shallow-draught Malay and Sumatran craft (Pires 1944, Vol. II, pp. 268–273; Reid 1993, pp. 36–43). `[FACT]`

### 1.2 Detailed Vessel Profiles

#### 1. Southeast Asian *Jong* (*djong* / *jung*)
- **`[FACT]` Not a Chinese junk:** Portuguese chroniclers called large Southeast Asian ships *juncos* (from Malay *jong* / Javanese *jung*), which led later historians to confuse them with Chinese junks. Pierre-Yves Manguin demonstrated that the *jong* was an indigenous Austronesian/Southeast Asian ocean-going vessel type distinct from Chinese ships (Manguin 1980, pp. 266–276).
- **`[FACT]` Construction:** Built with multiple layers of thick teak or tropical hardwood planks fastened edge-to-edge with wooden dowels (and lashed-lug framing in earlier/parallel traditions), with **no iron nails in the hull**; steered by **two quarter-rudders** (one on each side of the stern, occasionally with a third central rudder in hybrid accounts); rigged with two to four masts carrying woven-rattan *tanja* (canted rectangular) or mat-and-batten sails (Barbosa 1921, Vol. II, pp. 173–174; Manguin 1980, pp. 268–272).
- **`[FACT]` Size & Tonnage:** Manguin's survey of 16th-century Portuguese accounts gives an average burthen of **400–500 metric tons** (range 85 to 700+ tons; exceptional war *jongs* such as Pati Unus's 1513 flagship from Japara were reported at ~1,000 tons and towered over Albuquerque's 400-ton *Flor de la Mar*) (Manguin 1980, pp. 267–268; Manguin 1993, pp. 199–201).
- **`[FACT]` Governance on board:** The Melaka maritime code, *Undang-Undang Laut Melaka* (compiled under Sultan Mahmud Syah, r. 1488–1511), was written specifically for large trading *jongs*, granting the *nakhoda* (shipmaster) royal-like authority at sea (*nakhoda itu upama raja ia di laut*) and regulating space-sharing (*petak* cargo compartments) with traveling merchants (*kiwi*) (Manguin 2022, §20; Winstedt & de Josselin de Jong 1956).
- **`[FACT]` Who sailed them:** Major shipyards were on Java's north coast (*Pasisir* — Rembang, Demak, Japara, Tuban, Gresik) and in Pegu (lower Burma, rich in teak). Melaka itself owned and outfitted *jongs* under its sultans and resident merchants (Pires 1944, Vol. I, pp. 194–195; Vol. II, pp. 246–250).

#### 2. Chinese and Ryukyuan (*Lequios*) Junks
- **`[FACT]` Annual traffic:** Despite Ming maritime prohibitions (*haijin*), Chinese junks from Guangdong and Fujian called at Melaka (Pires notes that Guangzhou authorities charged steep fees for exit permits and restricted foreign junks to offshore anchorages like Tumon/Tuen Mun, ~30 leagues from Canton). Ryukyuan (*Lequios*) junks also sailed annually to Melaka (Pires 1944, Vol. I, pp. 119–131).
- **`[FACT]` Construction & Distinction:** Traditional Chinese ocean-going junks (*fuchuan*) featured iron-nailed hulls, transverse **watertight bulkheads**, a single **axial sternpost rudder**, and batten lug sails. By c. 1511, a hybrid "South China Sea tradition" had also emerged combining Southeast Asian teak/dowelled techniques with Chinese bulkheads and axial rudders (Manguin 1980, pp. 274–276; Manguin 1993, pp. 203–206).
- **`[FACT]` Size comparison:** Tomé Pires explicitly noted that Javanese and Malay *jongs* were much larger and more heavily built than the Chinese junks calling at Melaka (Pires 1944, Vol. I, p. 122).
- **`[FACT]` Customs privilege:** Chinese and Ryukyuan junks paid **presents** assessed through their *Syahbandar* (historically the *Laksamana* oversaw China, Ryukyu, Champa, and Cochinchina) rather than the standard 6% ad valorem duty charged to western Indian Ocean ships (Pires 1944, Vol. I, pp. 124–125; Vol. II, p. 273).

#### 3. Gujarati, Arab, Coromandel (Keling), and Bengali *Naus*
- **`[FACT]` Gujarat (Cambay):** Tomé Pires famously wrote that "Melaka cannot live without Cambay, nor Cambay without Melaka." **Four large ships (*naus*)** came every year from Gujarat to Melaka, each carrying merchandise worth **15,000 to 30,000 cruzados** (never less than 15,000), plus one ship directly from the city of Cambay; Pires reported ~1,000 resident Gujarati merchants and 4,000–5,000 Gujarati seamen passing through Melaka (Pires 1944, Vol. I, pp. 42–46; Vol. II, pp. 269–270; Wormser & Guillot 2011).
- **`[FACT]` Middle Eastern & East African merchants aboard Gujarati ships:** Merchants from Aden, Cairo, Mecca, Hormuz, Persia, Turkey, Armenia, and Swahili ports (Kilwa, Malindi, Mogadishu, Mombasa) formed commercial companies in Gujarat and sailed to Melaka aboard Gujarati *naus*, departing in March and returning via the Maldives (Pires 1944, Vol. I, p. 43; Vol. II, pp. 268–270).
- **`[FACT]` Coromandel (Keling) & Bengal:** From Coromandel, **3–4 ships** arrived annually worth **12,000–15,000 cruzados** each, plus **1–2 large ships or jongs from Pulicat** worth **80,000–90,000 cruzados** each—the single highest-value cargoes entering Melaka, paying 6% duty (Pires 1944, Vol. II, pp. 271–272). From Bengal, **4–5 ships** sailed yearly to Melaka and Pasai—both lighter Arab-style craft and 1–2 large *naus*/jongs carrying textiles and preserves worth **80,000–90,000 cruzados** (Pires 1944, Vol. I, pp. 92–93; Barbosa 1921, Vol. II, pp. 135–146).
- **`[FACT]`/`[INFERENCE]` Construction:** Western Indian Ocean ships (*dhow*-family and large *naus*) were typically built of Malabar teak laid carvel-style (edge-to-edge), traditionally **sewn with coir cordage** without iron nails (especially in the Arabian Sea and Malabar), steered by rope-controlled sternpost rudders, and rigged with **triangular lateen sails**; large Gujarati and Bengali *naus* often rivaled or exceeded Portuguese carracks in capacity (~300–600+ tons) (Barbosa 1921, Vol. II; Pearson 2003, pp. 62–75; Hourani 1995).

#### 4. *Lanchara* (*lancaran*), *Penjajap* (*pangajava*), and *Calaluz* (*kelulus*)
- **`[FACT]` *Lanchara*:** The workhorse regional vessel of the Malay Sultanate and Sumatra. Shallow-draught, propelled by both oars and 1–3 masts (*tanja* or lug sails), steered with twin quarter-rudders. Used both as **regional cargo vessels** (up to ~150 tons burthen) and as **warships** (with an elevated fighting deck, *balai*, swivel guns, and 150–200 crew) (Manguin 2012, pp. 148–165; Pires 1944, Vol. II).
- **`[FACT]` *Penjajap* (*pangajava*):** First recorded by Portuguese chronicler Fernão Lopes de Castanheda in 1509 as long, swift Sumatran vessels moving well under sail or oars; Afonso de Albuquerque recorded Malays defending Melaka in 1511 with *lancharas* and twenty *pangajaoas* (Castanheda 1552, Livro III, cap. 51; Albuquerque 1880, Vol. III). Primarily fast naval/patrol/raiding craft, though also capable of light urgent transport.
- **`[FACT]` *Calaluz* (*kelulus*):** Smaller, fast Malay/Javanese oared craft used for troop landings, harbour liaison, and coastal dispatch (Pires 1944; Albuquerque 1880, Vol. III).

### 1.3 Suggested Gameplay Abstractions — Vessels `[ABSTRACTION]`

- **Berth & Anchorage Sizing:** Group arriving ships into **3 berth classes** to keep harbour scheduling readable while preserving historical silhouettes:
  1. **Large Ocean-Going (Heavy Berth / Long Unload):** *Jong* (Javanese/Pegu/Malay), *Gujarati/Keling/Bengali Nau*, *Chinese/Ryukyuan Junk*. High cargo volume, high tax/royal-purchase value, slower unloading.
  2. **Regional Medium (Standard Berth / Medium Unload):** *Lanchara*, *Small Jong*, *Sambuk*. Frequent Straits/Sumatra/Peninsular traffic (tin, rice, pepper, regional goods).
  3. **Fast / Light (Quick Berth or Anchorage Tender):** *Penjajap*, *Calaluz*, *Perahu*. Used for urgent food/tin runs, diplomatic dispatches, harbour patrol, or smuggling events.
- **Deduction Hook (Origin vs. Vessel):** A ship's hull/rig type is a soft clue, not proof of guilt (`docs/gameplay-blueprint.md`, §06). For example, an Arab or Persian merchant legally arriving on a **Gujarati *nau*** is historically normal (Pires 1944, Vol. I, p. 43), whereas a manifest claiming "direct from Canton" on a coir-sewn lateen vessel with no bulkheads warrants closer questioning.
- **Unloading via Lighters:** Historically, large *jongs* and *naus* anchored offshore in the roadstead while *perahu* lighters ferried cargo across the river bar to the wharves and godowns (Pires 1944, Vol. II). Representing berths as "assigned unloading teams / wharfage slots" keeps the blueprint's 2-berth / 3-anchorage prototype historically grounded.

## 2. Weights and Measures in Melaka Trade (c. 1511)

### 2.1 The *Dacing* (*Dachem*) Steelyard and Dual-*Bahar* System

- **`[FACT]` Municipal Steelyard:** Heavy bulk commodities in Melaka were weighed on a suspended beam steelyard called the ***dacing*** (Portuguese *dachem*), overseen by port officials to standardize trade (Pires 1944, Vol. I, p. 101 n. 1; Nunes 1554/1868, p. 39).
- **`[FACT]` Two Different *Bahars* in the Same Port:** António Nunes's *Livro dos Pesos da Índia, e assi Medidas e Moedas* (1554, fol. 31 / ed. Felner 1868, p. 39)—corroborated by Tomé Pires and modern historians Paulo Jorge de Sousa Pinto (2012) and Daya Negri Wijaya (2022)—documents that Melaka used **two distinct *bahar* standards**, each divided into **200 *kati*** (*cates*), depending on the commodity:
  1. **Large *Bahar* (*bahar grande* / large *dachem*):** **200 *kati* = 210.29 kg** (`1 kati = 1.0514 kg`, i.e., 2 Portuguese *arráteis*, 4 *onças*, 5 *oitavas*, 15 *grãos*, 3 *dezavos*). Used for spices, aromatics, bulk metals, and dyes: **cloves, nutmeg, mace, pepper, sandalwood, frankincense/incense, myrrh, *pucho* (costus), brazilwood, iron, sulfur, saltpetre, oak galls** (Nunes 1554/1868, p. 39; Pinto 2012; Wijaya 2022).
  2. **Small *Bahar* (*bahar pequeno* / small *dachem*):** **200 *kati* = 183.60 kg** (`1 kati = 0.9180 kg`, i.e., 2 Portuguese *arráteis*). Used for **tin (*calaim* / pewter), China silk, ivory, opium / raw silk (*amfião* / *anafáia*), Chinese camphor, rosewater, liquid storax (*roçamalha*)**, and similar higher-value or denser goods (Nunes 1554/1868, p. 39; Pinto 2012; Wijaya 2022).
- **`[FACT]` Third *Kati* Standard for Precious Goods:** For **gold, musk, seed-pearls, coral, and *calambuco* (aloeswood)**, Melaka used a smaller precision *kati* of **20 *tahil* = 803.25 g** (28 Portuguese *onças*), making **1 *tahil* ≈ 40.16–41.23 g** (Nunes 1554/1868, p. 39; Yule & Burnell 1903, s.v. *Mace*; Pinto 2012).
- **`[FACT]` Contrast with the Chinese *Kati* and *Pikul*:** In Chinese and later Straits reckoning, 1 *kati* = 16 *tahil* ≈ **600–625 g**, and 100 *kati* = 1 *pikul* ≈ **60–62.5 kg** (with 3 *pikul* ≈ 180–187.5 kg roughly matching the Small *Bahar*) (Borschberg 2015; Wijaya 2022). Thus, three different *kati* weights (~605 g Chinese, ~803 g Melaka precious, ~918 g Melaka small *dacing*, ~1,051 g Melaka large *dacing*) existed in the Melaka trading world!

### 2.3 Volume and Count Measures

- **`[FACT]` Volume (Rice, Grain, Salt, Oils):** Indigenous Malay and Javanese dry trade used capacity measures (*takar*) rather than weight:
  - **4 *chupak*** (coconut-shell/bamboo measure, ~1.14 L) = **1 *gantang*** (*ganta*) (Crawfurd 1856, p. 446).
  - **1 *gantang*** varied regionally (~3.0 L in Portuguese/Philippine *ganta* reckoning up to ~4.55 L in later Malay standard; Nunes 1554, p. 39 equates 1 Goa *candy* to **140 *gantas*** at Melaka) (Nunes 1554/1868, p. 39; Yule & Burnell 1903, s.v. *Ganton*).
  - **1 *koyan*** = **800 *gantang*** (later standardized as 40 *pikul* ≈ 2.4 metric tons of rice). The *Undang-Undang Laut Melaka* already uses *koyan* as the standard bulk unit for grain cargo shares allotted to *kiwi* merchants aboard *jongs* (Crawfurd 1856, p. 446; Winstedt & de Josselin de Jong 1956).
- **`[FACT]` Count (Cloth / Textiles):** Indian and Southeast Asian textiles were universally traded and priced by the ***kodi*** (Portuguese ***corge*** / ***corja***) = **20 pieces (a score)** (Pires 1944, Vol. I–II; Barbosa 1921, Vol. II).
- **`[INFERENCE]` Count / Lot (Porcelain, Glassware, Timber):** Porcelain and glassware were traded by count, nested bundles, or jar-lots (large martaban storage jars often packed with nested bowls—`docs/gameplay-blueprint.md`, §07), while timber was traded by piece/log or ship-load.

### 2.5 Suggested Gameplay Abstractions — Weights & Measures `[ABSTRACTION]`

- **Keep 4 Clean Player-Facing Units in Manifests & the Manual:**
  1. ***Bahar*** (bulk weight — with the Reference Manual noting **Large *Bahar* ~210 kg** for spices/iron/sandalwood/frankincense vs. **Small *Bahar* ~184 kg** for tin/silk/copper).
  2. ***Kati*** (sub-unit for smaller lots; 200 *kati* = 1 *bahar*).
  3. ***Koyan* / *Gantang*** (bulk grain/rice).
  4. ***Kodi*** (bundles of 20 cloths; plus piece/jar lots for porcelain, glassware, timber).
- **High-Value Deduction Mechanic (Without Tedious Math):** Do not force players to do decimal conversions in real time (`docs/gameplay-blueprint.md`, §14 — weighing puzzles are exploratory backlog). Instead, use the **Large vs. Small *Dacing*** rule as a crisp Reference Manual lookup: if a pepper manifest declares "10 *bahar*" but the physical cargo was weighed on the **Small *dacing*** (or declared in Chinese *kati* of ~605 g), the actual mass is ~13%–40% short—a historically authentic customs discrepancy!

## 3. Currencies, Money of Account, and Exchange Values (c. 1511)

### 3.1 Pre-Conquest Melaka Sultanate Monetary System (Before August 1511)

- **`[FACT]` Official Local Coinage — Tin *Caixas* and *Calaim*:**
  - The Melaka Sultanate minted small cast **tin coins** (*pitis* / *keping* in Malay; called ***caixas*** or ***caixes*** by the Portuguese, likened to Portuguese *ceitis*), inscribed in Jawi with the reigning Sultan's name from Sultan Muzaffar Syah (r. 1445–1459) onward (Albuquerque 1557/1880, Vol. III, p. 77; Hanitsch 1903, pp. 183–189; Shaw & Mohd Kassim Haji Ali 1970).
  - **100 *caixas* = 1 *calaim*** (the standard unit of tin money/account in Melaka, also used for cast tin bars/blocks of standard weight; `1 calaim` was valued by Portuguese law/reckoning at **11 *réis* and 4 *ceitis***, i.e., ~11.67 *réis*) (Albuquerque 1557/1880, Vol. III, p. 77; Correia 1858–1866; Hanitsch 1903, p. 188).
  - Tin *caixas* and *calains* were used for everyday bazaar purchases (food, wages, small dues) and even tribute (Pires records neighbouring vassals paying annual tribute in thousands of *calains*, e.g., 4,000 *calains*/year) (Pires 1944, Vol. II; Correia in Birch 1880, Vol. III, p. 139 n. 1).
- **`[FACT]` No Native Gold or Silver Coinage:** Both Brás de Albuquerque's *Commentaries* (1557/1880, Vol. III, pp. 77, 138) and Gaspar Correia's *Lendas da Índia* explicitly report that the Sultanate of Melaka struck **no gold or silver coins of its own**. Gold (especially Sumatran/Minangkabau and Pahang gold dust) and silver passed **by weight** (in *tahil* and *mas*) as bullion/merchandise (Albuquerque 1557/1880, Vol. III, p. 77; Da Cunha 1880, p. 11; Wicks 1992).
- **`[FACT]` Wholesale Settlement — Barter, Credit, and Bullion:** Large international cargoes (e.g., Gujarati textiles for Moluccan spices or Chinese silk) were settled through a mix of **valuated barter** (goods priced in a shared unit of account and exchanged), **weighed gold/silver bullion**, and **foreign coins**—confirming the blueprint's rule that not all trade should be modeled as simple barter (`docs/gameplay-blueprint.md`, §07; Pires 1944, Vol. I–II; Wicks 1992; Singh 2009).
- **`[FACT]` Foreign Currencies Circulating in Melaka:**
  - **Chinese copper cash (*caixas* / *pitis* / *wen*):** Widely used across Java (Demak/Majapahit) and Nusantara and present in Melaka trade (Pires 1944, Vol. I; Barreto 2007; Wicks 1992).
  - **Pasai & Pedir gold coins (*dramas* / *dirhams*):** Gold coins from north Sumatra circulated in Melaka alongside Melaka's tin coinage (Pires 1944, Vol. I; Wicks 1992).
  - **Bengali silver *tanka* (*tamcat*):** Weighed ½ *tael* (~20 g) and was worth **20 *calains* at Melaka** (Pires/Ramusio *Sommario*, 1512–1515; Atlas of Money).
  - **Hormuz silver *tangas*:** Worth ~62–69 *réis* in Portuguese reckoning (Pires 1944, Vol. I).
  - **Pegu *ganza*:** Uncoined copper-lead alloy lumps used in Pegu, known to Melaka merchants (Nunes 1554/1868, p. 38; Yule & Burnell 1903, s.v. *Ganza*).
  - **Maldivian cowries:** Exported via Melaka to Bengal, Pegu, and Siam (where 1 *calaim* bought ~458–1,500 cowries, or one good hen), though cowries did **not** serve as Melaka's own local currency (Pires/Ramusio *Sommario*; Yang 2016, p. 13).

### 3.2 The Portuguese *Cruzado* as Money of Account

- **`[FACT]` Gold vs. Silver *Cruzado*:** Tomé Pires and Duarte Barbosa express virtually all Melaka cargo values, prices, and customs valuations in Portuguese ***cruzados***.
  - In Portugal and Portuguese Asia generally, 1 gold *cruzado* = **400 *réis*** (~3.55 g fine gold, equivalent to a Venetian ducat) (Wijaya 2022; Boxer 1968; Pinto 2012).
  - In Portuguese Malacca specifically, silver *cruzados* were commonly reckoned at **360 *réis*** (Pinto 2012; Boxer 1968; Wijaya 2022).
- **`[FACT]` Purchasing Power Anchors (c. 1511–1515):**
  - 1 *calaim* (100 tin *caixas*) ≈ **1 good hen** in regional reckoning (~11.67 *réis*) (Pires/Ramusio *Sommario*; Albuquerque 1557/1880, Vol. III, p. 77).
  - 1 *quintal* (~58.75 kg) of Malabar/Sumatran pepper bought in Melaka ≈ **4 *cruzados*** (selling in China for 15–16 *cruzados*) (Pires 1944, Vol. I; ICM Macau 1996).
  - 1 Gujarati *nau* cargo ≈ **15,000–30,000 *cruzados***; 1 Pulicat (Keling) or large Bengali *nau* cargo ≈ **80,000–90,000 *cruzados*** (Pires 1944, Vol. II, pp. 269–272).
  - Melaka Muslim marriage dowry (for *Moors*) = **10 *tahil* and 6 *mas* of gold** (Pires 1944, Vol. II).

### 3.3 Post-August 1511 Monetary Shock — Albuquerque's Mint

`[FACT]` Immediately after conquering Melaka in August 1511, Afonso de Albuquerque—at the request of the Hindu merchant leader **Nina Chatu** (*Ninachatu*) and local governors to relieve currency disruption—established a Portuguese mint (the only European mint ever set up in Melaka) and ordered all Sultanate tin *caixas* turned in for reminting under pain of death (Albuquerque 1557/1880, Vol. III, pp. 137–139; Hanitsch 1903, pp. 188–189; Atlas of Money):
- **Gold:** *Catholico* (¼ *tundia* = **1,000 *réis***, i.e., 2.5 gold *cruzados*).
- **Silver:** *Malaqueses* (¼ *tundia* value; deliberately overvalued by Albuquerque to attract Portuguese silver).
- **Tin (pewter):** *Dinheiro* (= 2 old *caixas*, stamped with King Manuel's armillary sphere), *Soldo* (= 10 *dinheiros*), and *Bastardo* (= 10 *soldos* = 100 *dinheiros*). Crown took direct ownership of the tin mines.

### 3.5 Suggested Gameplay Abstractions — Currency `[ABSTRACTION]`

- **Dual-Layer Economy Display:**
  - **Treasury & Wholesale Ledger:** Use **Gold *Tahil* (and *Mas*)** (for authentic pre-1511 Sultanate immersion: `1 tahil = 16 mas`) OR ***Cruzados*** (if the team prefers a single decimal-friendly unit familiar from Portuguese sources). **Recommendation:** Display Sultanate treasury in ***Tahil* (Gold)** (or ***Calains*** for small port expenses like staff wages/berth upkeep), with **1 *tahil* gold ≈ 10 *cruzados* ≈ 300–340 *calains*** as a clean gameplay conversion.
  - **Flavor & Inspection Evidence:** Keep foreign currencies (**Chinese copper cash**, **Pasai gold *dramas***, **Bengali *tankas***, **Portuguese *cruzados***, and **weighed gold dust**) as manifest/dialogue flavor and inspection clues rather than forcing the player to manage 6 separate wallets.
  - **1511 Climax Hook:** If the campaign reaches August 1511, Albuquerque's forced recall of Sultanate tin *caixas* and minting of *dinheiros*/*soldos*/*bastardos*/*Malaqueses* provides a stark, historically grounded late-game economic shock.

## 4. Quick-Reference Inspection & Deduction Hooks for the Writer `[ABSTRACTION]`

| Discrepancy Type | Historical Mechanism (Sourced) | Gameplay Clue in Detailed Inspection |
|---|---|---|
| **Wrong *Dacing* (Steelyard)** | Pepper/nutmeg/iron must use the **Large *bahar*** (210.3 kg), whereas tin/silk use the **Small *bahar*** (183.6 kg) (Nunes 1554/1868, p. 39). `[FACT]` | Manifest claims "12 *bahar* of pepper," but the steelyard weight tags match the Small *dacing* (~13% underweight—either honest port confusion or deliberate tax/volume skimming). |
| **Chinese vs. Melaka *Kati*** | A Chinese *kati* (~605 g) is ~35–42% lighter than Melaka's Small/Large *kati* (918–1,051 g) (Borschberg 2015; Nunes 1554/1868, p. 39). `[FACT]` | A Fujian junk's manifest lists silk or copper in *kati* without converting to Melaka's *dacing*, causing a major weight mismatch on the wharf. |
| **Cloth Count (*Kodi*) Shorting** | Indian textiles are bundled in ***kodi* (*corges*) of 20 pieces** (Pires 1944; Barbosa 1921). `[FACT]` | Opening a sealed bale declared as "10 *kodi*" reveals 18 pieces per bundle—or cheaper coarse Keling cloth hidden inside fine Cambay outer wraps. |
| **Vessel–Origin Mismatch** | Southeast Asian *jongs* have dowelled hulls, no iron nails, and twin quarter-rudders; Chinese junks have iron nails, bulkheads, and axial rudders; Arab/Gujarati ships use sewn/carvel hulls and lateen sails (Manguin 1980; Pearson 2003). `[FACT]` | A captain claims to have sailed direct from Canton, yet the vessel is a twin-rudder Javanese *jong* with no bulkheads—suggesting transshipment via Pasai or Demak (which may explain missing or altered seals, not necessarily guilt). |
| **Bullion Fineness & Foreign Coin** | Melaka had no native gold/silver coin; high-value payments used weighed gold dust (*tahil*/*mas*) or foreign coins like Bengali *tankas* (20 *calains*) (Albuquerque 1557/1880; Pires/Ramusio). `[FACT]` | A merchant offers clipped foreign coins or low-touch gold dust for customs duties, or declares goods in Javanese *tundia* (which Pires notes differed by a quarter from Melaka's *tahil*) to underpay duty. |
