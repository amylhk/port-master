# Melaka, 1509–1511: Sourced Historical Timeline, Actors, Disputed Points, and Gameplay Hooks

**Research Document for *Port Master: Melaka 1511***  
**Scope:** Diogo Lopes de Sequeira's 1509 visit, the Portuguese captives, the 1510 court crisis, Afonso de Albuquerque's arrival, the July–August 1511 siege and fall, key political and commercial actors, immediate post-conquest port aftermath (late 1511–1512+), and historiographical disagreements.  
**Related Design Doc:** [`docs/gameplay-blueprint.md`](../gameplay-blueprint.md) (Concept Document v0.5)

---

## Epistemological Key

Every claim in this document is explicitly marked to separate primary historical evidence from modern interpretation and game-design speculation:

- **`[Documented Fact]`** — Directly attested in contemporary or near-contemporary primary sources (Portuguese royal instructions, letters, chronicles, *Suma Oriental*, *Sejarah Melayu*, or *Ming Shilu*). Where primary sources conflict on dates, numbers, or motives, the divergence is flagged inline and analyzed in [Section 4](#4-where-the-sources-disagree).
- **`[Scholarly Inference]`** — Causal interpretation, structural analysis, or source-critical synthesis by named modern historians (e.g., Meilink-Roelofsz, Thomaz, Subrahmanyam, Andaya & Andaya, Reid, Borschberg, Pinto).
- **`[Gameplay Hook]`** — Non-canonical design suggestion linking the historical record to *Port Master: Melaka 1511* mechanics (berthing priority, Detailed Inspection, Evening Intelligence, Royal First Purchase, anchorage congestion, and the 1511 finale).

> **Calendar note:** All European dates below follow the **Julian calendar** in use in 1509–1511, as recorded in Portuguese primary sources. Malay sources (*Sejarah Melayu*) generally omit calendar dates, requiring cross-referencing with Portuguese records (Winstedt 1935; Brown 1952; Andaya & Andaya 2017).

## 1. Chronological Timeline (1508–1512)

### Phase I — Portuguese Preparation & Sequeira's 1509 Visit

#### 1506 — Early Portuguese Planning for a Melaka Fortress
- **`[Documented Fact]`** In 1506, King Manuel I of Portugal already wrote to India mentioning that he was sending "half of a wooden fort" and artillery from Lisbon to be erected in Melaka at a spot "strong and healthy, with a good harbour" that "should allow the commerce of merchandise" (King Manuel I's 1506 letter, cited in Loureiro, "Historical Notes on the Portuguese Fortress of Malacca").
- **`[Scholarly Inference]`** Portuguese interest in Melaka predated Sequeira's 1509 ambush; the Crown aimed from the outset to insert fortified factories (*feitorias*) into Asia's principal spice-trade chokepoints (Loureiro; Subrahmanyam 1993; Pinto 2013).
- **`[Gameplay Hook]`** *Early-game rumors:* Evening intelligence from Gujarati or Calicut captains can carry rumors of Portuguese forts in Malabar and rumors that the "White Bengalis" (*Bengali putih*) are asking about Melaka well before their first sails appear.

#### 13 February & April 1508 — Royal Instructions (*Regimento*) and Departure of Sequeira
- **`[Documented Fact]`** On 13 February 1508, King Manuel I issued the *Almeirim Regimento* to Diogo Lopes de Sequeira, ordering him to sail with four ships to Madagascar and Melaka, negotiate a peaceful trading post, avoid provocation ("dissimulate it as best you can, showing that you wish not but peace and friendship"), and gather systematic intelligence on Chinese merchants, their ships, goods, weaponry, and religion (Letter/Regimento of King Manuel I to Sequeira, Feb. 1508, quoted in Abreu 1988; Castanheda, *História*, Book II, ch. 109; Barros, *Décadas*, II, iv, 3).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Sequeira sailed from Lisbon in April 1508, reached Cochin in April 1509, where Viceroy Dom Francisco de Almeida added a fifth ship (a carrack carrying several Albuquerque partisans, including Ferdinand Magellan and Francisco Serrão), and then sailed east, calling peacefully at Pedir and Pasai in northern Sumatra and erecting stone crosses (*padrões*) there (Castanheda, Book II, ch. 111–113; Barros, II, iv, 3; Correia, *Lendas*, Tomo II).
- **`[Gameplay Hook]`** *Pre-arrival intelligence:* Ships arriving from Pedir and Pasai can report five strange European vessels asking questions about Chinese junks and harbour defenses—giving the player a morning briefing warning before Sequeira's fleet enters the anchorage.

#### 11 September 1509 — Sequeira Arrives at Melaka
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Diogo Lopes de Sequeira anchored off Melaka on **11 September 1509** (some accounts place arrival in August–September 1509; see [Section 4.1](#41-dates-of-sequeiras-arrival-and-the-1509-ambush)) with five ships, firing cannon salutes that alarmed the harbour and prompted local vessels to prepare for defense (Castanheda, Book II, ch. 114; Barros, II, iv, 3–4; Correia, Tomo II).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Initial reception was formally hospitable. Sequeira sent Jerónimo Teixeira ashore with royal gifts; Sultan Mahmud Shah and Bendahara Tun Mutahir (Bendahara Seri Maharaja) received the embassy, granted permission to trade, and assigned a house/factory ashore for Rui de Araújo and his men to buy cloves and spices (Castanheda, Book II, ch. 114–115; Barros, II, iv, 4; *Commentaries*, Part III, ch. 19).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Four Chinese junks were in port at the time; Castanheda notes that the Portuguese and Chinese captains got along so well that Sequeira dined aboard the Chinese junks the next day (Castanheda, Book II, ch. 114; Abreu 1988).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** *Sejarah Melayu* records the arrival of the Portuguese ("Peranggi" / "Feringgi"), whom Melakans called *Bengali putih* ("white Bengalis"), marveling at their appearance, clothing, and cannons, and recounting an anecdote of the Portuguese captain placing a gold chain around Bendahara Tun Mutahir's neck, which courtiers viewed as an affront to Malay etiquette though the Bendahara brushed it off (*Sejarah Melayu*, Raffles MS 18, trans. Brown 1952, ch. 22–23).
- **`[Gameplay Hook]`** *Berthing & protocol crisis:* Sequeira's five armed ships arrive firing salutes. Granting them a berth and shore warehouse boosts immediate spice sales and pleases Chinese captains, but triggers fierce protests from Gujarati and Muslim merchant guilds in the Evening phase.

#### Late September / October 1509 — The Ambush of Sequeira and Capture of the Factory
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Gujarati and other Muslim merchants—who knew of Portuguese naval warfare against Muslim shipping in the Indian Ocean (including Almeida's victory at Diu in February 1509)—lobbied the Sultan and Bendahara Tun Mutahir to destroy the Portuguese before they could fortify Melaka (Barros, II, iv, 4; Castanheda, Book II, ch. 115–116; *Commentaries*, III, ch. 19; Pires, *Suma Oriental*, Book VI, Cortesão trans. II, pp. 254–255).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** A plot was hatched to invite Sequeira and his captains to a banquet ashore while Malay *perahu* surrounded the Portuguese ships under the pretext of loading cloves. Accounts differ on who leaked the warning (a Javanese woman connected to Utimutiraja's household, or Chinese/Keling contacts; see [Section 4.2](#42-who-instigated-and-who-betrayed-the-1509-ambush)). Sequeira—playing chess on deck when the signal was given—cut his anchor cables and escaped with his ships, though Portuguese chroniclers credit Ferdinand Magellan with rowing to warn Sequeira or rescue Francisco Serrão ashore (Castanheda, Book II, ch. 116–117; Barros, II, iv, 4; Correia, Tomo II).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Roughly **19 to 30 Portuguese** (including factor **Rui de Araújo**) were left ashore, killed or captured, and their merchandise seized. Sequeira burned two ships of his own that lacked crew (or seized/burned local vessels in reprisal) and sailed back to India, reaching Cochin in April 1510 before departing directly for Portugal to avoid Albuquerque (Castanheda, Book II, ch. 117–118; Barros, II, iv, 4; Correia, Tomo II).
- **`[Scholarly Inference]`** The 1509 clash ended any realistic prospect of a purely commercial Portuguese factory in Melaka and supplied Albuquerque with a legal and political *casus belli*—the recovery of royal subjects and goods—to justify military conquest (Subrahmanyam 1993; Thomaz 2000; Pinto 2013).
- **`[Gameplay Hook]`** *Detailed Inspection & Intelligence:* Suspicious *perahu* approaching anchored ships under a "clove-loading" manifest carry concealed weapons. A player who conducts a Detailed Inspection or follows an Evening tip from a Javanese or Keling contact can uncover the ambush plot—forcing a choice between alerting the Bendahara, warning the Portuguese, or letting events play out.

### Phase II — The Portuguese Captives & Melaka's 1510 Political Crisis

#### Late 1509 – Early 1511 — The Portuguese Captives and Rui de Araújo's Smuggled Intelligence
- **`[Documented Fact]`** The surviving captives (~19–20 men led by factor Rui de Araújo) were imprisoned in Melaka. In a smuggled letter dated **6 February 1510** addressed to Afonso de Albuquerque (carried via emissaries of the wealthy Tamil Hindu merchant **Nina Chatu**), Araújo described their harsh captivity—including pressure and torture to force them to convert to Islam and teach the Malays how to cast cannon and make gunpowder—and provided detailed military intelligence: Melaka had roughly 10,000 houses (about 500 of stone/clay), ~90–100 junks anchored in port at any time (~20 local, plus ~150 royal *perahu*), ~4,000 fighting men, and artillery that was poorly served for lack of gunners and good powder; Araújo urged Albuquerque to come in force to conquer the city (Rui de Araújo to Albuquerque, 6 Feb. 1510, in *Cartas de Afonso de Albuquerque*, Vol. III, pp. 5–11; cited in Loureiro; Borschberg 2019; Pinto 2013).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Several captives managed to escape to Pedir in northern Sumatra (including João Viegas and companions), while Nina Chatu—a prominent Keling (Tamil Hindu) merchant from the Coromandel network—secretly fed and protected Araújo's group in Melaka and maintained a clandestine correspondence channel to Portuguese India (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 19–20; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 50–51; Thomaz 1976, 1985).
- **`[Scholarly Inference]`** Nina Chatu's aid was not altruistic: Keling Hindu merchants were losing court favor to Gujarati and Tamil-Muslim factions under Sultan Mahmud and Bendahara Tun Mutahir, and saw a Portuguese intervention as a chance to displace their Gujarati rivals (Thomaz 1976, 1985; Subrahmanyam 1986; Pinto 2013).
- **`[Gameplay Hook]`** *Clandestine correspondence:* During the 1510 mid-game, a Keling vessel departing for Coromandel or Pedir carries a concealed letter inside a porcelain jar or cloth bale. Discovering it during Detailed Inspection lets the player decide whether to confiscate it for the Sultan, blackmail Nina Chatu, or let it pass.

#### c. 1510 — Execution of Bendahara Tun Mutahir and Succession Crisis
- **`[Documented Fact]`** In **1510**, Sultan Mahmud Shah ordered the execution of **Bendahara Seri Maharaja Tun Mutahir** along with his son Tun Hassan (the Temenggong), his brother Seri Nara Diraja Tun Tahir, and his son-in-law Tun Ali—sparing only his daughter **Tun Fatimah** (whom the Sultan then married) and a young boy, Tun Hamzah (*Sejarah Melayu*, Raffles MS 18, Brown trans. ch. 22; Pires, *Suma Oriental*, Cortesão II, p. 255; *Commentaries*, III, ch. 19; Barros, II, vi, 1).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** *Sejarah Melayu* attributes the execution to a court intrigue involving the wealthy Tamil-Muslim merchant/shahbandar **Raja Mendeliar**, his debtor **Si Kitul**, and **Laksamana Khoja Hassan**, who falsely accused Tun Mutahir of plotting to seize the throne—compounded by Sultan Mahmud's personal resentment that Tun Mutahir had married his beautiful daughter Tun Fatimah to Tun Ali without first offering her to the Sultan (*Sejarah Melayu*, Brown trans. ch. 22). Portuguese sources instead emphasize Tun Mutahir's immense wealth, arrogance, and alleged conspiracy against the Sultan—some claiming Tun Mutahir had been the chief instigator of the 1509 attack on Sequeira, after which the Sultan blamed and purged him (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 19; Pires, Cortesão II, pp. 254–255; see [Section 4.3](#43-why-bendahara-tun-mutahir-was-executed-in-1510)).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** To replace Tun Mutahir, Sultan Mahmud forced the office of Bendahara upon the elderly, toothless, and crippled **Paduka Tuan (Tun Tepok / Bendahara Lubuk Batu)**, son of the great Bendahara Tun Perak, who protested his infirmity and had to be carried into the audience hall in a litter (*Sejarah Melayu*, Brown trans. ch. 22–23).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Shortly thereafter—deeply remorseful over killing Tun Mutahir and later executing Raja Mendeliar and Si Kitul—Sultan Mahmud **abdicated in favour of his son, Sultan Ahmad Shah**, retreating temporarily from active governance, though he retained immense behind-the-scenes influence (*Sejarah Melayu*, Brown trans. ch. 23; Pires, Cortesão II, p. 255).
- **`[Scholarly Inference]`** The 1510 purge decapitated Melaka's political and administrative leadership less than a year before Albuquerque's invasion, shattering the delicate Malay–Tamil-Muslim–Keling court coalition that Bendahara Tun Perak (d. 1498) had built and leaving the sultanate divided between father (Mahmud), son (Ahmad), an incapacitated Bendahara, and alienated merchant elites (Winstedt 1935; Andaya & Andaya 2017; Hashim 1992; Thomaz 1993).
- **`[Gameplay Hook]`** *Court shock event:* The 1510 Bendahara purge hits port administration directly—sudden shifts in royal purchasing orders, frozen merchant credit, fearful Tamil-Muslim and Keling factions, and an incapacitated Bendahara who can no longer resolve guild disputes quickly, pushing heavier administrative decisions onto the player.

#### August 1510 – February 1511 — Vasconcelos Detoured; Albuquerque Takes Goa and Plans for Melaka
- **`[Documented Fact]`** In April 1510, King Manuel I dispatched **Diogo Mendes de Vasconcelos** with four ships directly to trade at Melaka, assuming Sequeira had succeeded. Arriving at Angediva in August 1510, Vasconcelos was detained by Governor **Afonso de Albuquerque**, who used the fleet in his second, decisive capture of **Goa (25 November 1510)** and refused to let Vasconcelos sail to Melaka alone (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 1–10; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 43–49; Correia, Tomo II).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** After an aborted attempt to sail to the Red Sea in early 1511 (blocked by the monsoon), Albuquerque diverted his entire armada toward Melaka to free the captives and capture the straits entrepôt (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 14–15; Subrahmanyam 1993).

### Phase III — Albuquerque's Voyage & Arrival (April – July 1511)

#### Late April / 2 May 1511 — Albuquerque Sails from Cochin; Calls at Pedir and Pasai
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Albuquerque departed Cochin in late April / early May 1511 (sources vary between 20 April and 2 May) with **16 to 19 ships** carrying roughly **700–800 Portuguese soldiers** and **200–300 Malabar/Indian auxiliaries** (plus enslaved rowers/attendants, bringing total personnel to ~1,100–1,400; see [Section 4.5](#45-force-sizes-artillery-counts-and-casualties)) (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 15; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 50; Barros, II, vi, 2; Correia, Tomo II).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** En route, the fleet captured Gujarati merchant ships and called at **Pedir** (where escaped captives including **João Viegas** joined Albuquerque with fresh intelligence) and **Pasai** (where Albuquerque encountered **Nakhoda Beguea**, a Malay/Gujarati captain implicated in the 1509 plot, and the deposed claimant Zayn al-Din) (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 16–18; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 51–53; Barros, II, vi, 2).
- **`[Gameplay Hook]`** *Blockade warning:* A damaged Gujarati ship limps into Melaka's anchorage reporting that a massive Portuguese war fleet just seized vessels off Pedir and Pasai. Commodity prices for western cloth spike while ships try to depart early.

#### 1 July 1511 — Albuquerque Anchors Off Melaka
- **`[Documented Fact]`** On **1 July 1511** (some accounts say mid-June; see [Section 4.4](#44-dates-of-albuquerques-arrival-the-second-assault-and-the-fall-of-melaka)), Albuquerque's armada sailed into Melaka's roadstead dressed in battle banners and firing heavy cannon salutes. He immediately proclaimed that no vessel in the harbour could depart without his permission (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 20; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 53; Barros, II, vi, 2).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Five (or six) **Chinese junk captains** in port—who claimed Sultan Mahmud had detained their ships and forced them to serve in his war against Aru—immediately visited Albuquerque to offer alliance and assistance. Albuquerque declined to risk their lives in the main assault but accepted the loan of their landing boats and intelligence, and later sent his envoy **Duarte Fernandes** to Ayutthaya (Siam) aboard a departing Chinese junk (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 20, 26; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 53; Barros, II, vi, 3).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Through **Nina Chatu**, Rui de Araújo continued smuggling intelligence out to Albuquerque's flagship (*Flor de la Mar*), warning him not to trust the Sultan's stalling tactics and estimating Melaka's defenses. Conversely, Albuquerque later wrote to King Manuel that Sultan Mahmud had agents count the Portuguese soldiers accurately to within "fewer than three men," making the Sultan overconfident in his numerical superiority (*Cartas de Afonso de Albuquerque*, Vol. I; *Commentaries*, III, ch. 20–21).
- **`[Gameplay Hook]`** *Harbour lockdown:* Normal daily traffic freezes into a siege economy. The player must manage trapped foreign junks (Chinese captains demanding clearance to leave, Gujarati ships seeking protection, Javanese rice boats negotiating neutrality) while food reserves dwindle.

#### Early – Mid-July 1511 — Stalled Negotiations, Waterfront Burning, and Release of Captives
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Albuquerque demanded the immediate, unconditional release of Rui de Araújo and all Portuguese captives plus restitution of Sequeira's confiscated goods. Sultan Mahmud stalled—sending envoys to propose a peace treaty first—while fortifying the city's wooden palisades, positioning artillery along the river and bridge, and summoning his fleet under the **Laksamana** (stationed near Singapore/Riau; see Borschberg 2019) (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 20–21; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 53–54; Barros, II, vi, 3).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Losing patience in mid-July, Albuquerque sent armed boats to burn Gujarati ships in the harbour and houses along the waterfront, sparing the vessels of non-hostile merchants (Chinese, Keling, Pegu). Alarmed by the fire, Sultan Mahmud promptly released Rui de Araújo and the surviving Portuguese captives (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 21; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 54; Barros, II, vi, 3).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Once the captives were safe aboard his fleet, Albuquerque escalated his demands: **300,000 cruzados** (or 50,000–300,000 depending on chronicler) in war indemnity and permission to build a **stone fortress** wherever he chose in Melaka. Sultan Mahmud refused, making war inevitable (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 21; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 55; Correia, Tomo II).
- **`[Scholarly Inference]`** Scholars largely agree Albuquerque never intended to settle for a mere trading post; the escalated demands were designed to force a rejection and justify outright conquest (Pinto 2013; Subrahmanyam 1993; Thomaz 2000).
- **`[Gameplay Hook]`** *Diplomatic crisis:* The player's warehouse and berths face direct arson risk. Choosing whether to prioritize firefighting on Gujarati warehouses or Keling/Javanese grain stores directly alters which merchant communities stay loyal or defect during the assault.

### Phase IV — The Siege and Fall of Melaka (25 July – Late August 1511)

#### 25 July 1511 (St. James's Day) — First Portuguese Assault on the Bridge
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Recognizing that Melaka was bisected by the **Melaka River**—with the royal palace, main mosque, and Malay town (*Bandar Melaka*) on the south/southeast bank and the commercial merchant quarters (*Upeh* / *Tranquerah*) on the north/northwest bank, linked by a single wide wooden **bridge**—Albuquerque aimed his attack directly at the bridge to split the defenders (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 22; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 55; Barros, II, vi, 4; Thomaz 2000).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** At dawn on **25 July 1511** (Feast of St. James the Greater), Albuquerque landed in two divisions—one under **Dom João de Lima** attacking the Upeh (north) side of the bridge, the other under **Albuquerque himself** attacking the mosque/palace (south) side. They stormed the stockades and seized the bridge after bitter street fighting, facing heavy fire, war elephants (reportedly led by Sultan Mahmud and Sultan Ahmad), and poisoned blowpipe darts (*sumpitan*) and arrows (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 22–23; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 56; Barros, II, vi, 4).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Although the Portuguese burned part of the town and royal quarter and held the bridge through the day, heat exhaustion, lack of food/water, mounting casualties from poisoned darts, and inability to fortify the bridge before nightfall forced Albuquerque to withdraw his troops back to the ships at dusk (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 23; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 56–57; Correia, Tomo II).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** *Sejarah Melayu* recounts that on the night before the decisive Portuguese attack, young Malay nobles asked Sultan Ahmad for the *Hikayat Muhammad Hanafiah* to inspire their courage; the Sultan first offered the *Hikayat Amir Hamzah* before relenting and lending *Muhammad Hanafiah* (*Sejarah Melayu*, Raffles MS 18, Brown trans. ch. 23; Daneshgar 2018).
- **`[Gameplay Hook]`** *Bridge choke-point:* The river bridge separates the state warehouses/palace from the merchant quarters of Upeh. Once fighting begins on 25 July, goods and messages can no longer cross freely between the two banks; player decisions made earlier on where to stockpile rice and silver suddenly determine which side can hold out.

#### 26 July – 9 August 1511 — The Tall Armed Junk and Merchant Defections
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Between the two assaults, Albuquerque converted a **tall, high-sided junk** (either seized earlier or provided with local/Chinese assistance) into a floating siege tower—fortified with barrels, timber, sandbags, mantlets, and field artillery under **António de Abreu**—designed to overtop the bridge's palisades when floated upriver on the spring tide (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 24; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 58; Barros, II, vi, 5).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Meanwhile, **Utimutiraja** (head of the powerful Javanese community in Upeh) and **Nina Chatu** (head of the Keling merchants), along with Burmese (Pegu) traders, negotiated secretly with Albuquerque, promising neutrality in exchange for Portuguese safe-conduct banners (*bandeiras*) to hang over their houses and warehouses to spare them from looting (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 24–25; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 58; Barros, II, vi, 5; Thomaz 1985).
- **`[Scholarly Inference]`** The neutrality and quiet defection of Melaka's largest non-Malay merchant communities—especially Utimutiraja's several thousand Javanese dependents in Upeh—critically undermined Sultan Mahmud's ability to defend the north bank (Meilink-Roelofsz 1962; Andaya & Andaya 2017; Reid 1993).
- **`[Gameplay Hook]`** *Evening crisis of loyalty:* During the two-week lull, Utimutiraja and Nina Chatu sound out the player. Does the player enforce royal requisitioning of merchant warehouses (risking immediate defection) or cut backroom deals to keep food flowing?

#### 10 August 1511 (St. Lawrence's Day) — Second Assault and Capture of the Bridge
- **`[Documented Fact]`** On **10 August 1511** (dated 8 August by R. S. Whiteway; see [Section 4.4](#44-dates-of-albuquerques-arrival-the-second-assault-and-the-fall-of-melaka)), António de Abreu worked the fortified junk upriver on the high tide until it grounded against the bridge, pouring cannon and matchlock fire down onto the Malay defenders despite Abreu being severely wounded in the jaw (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 25; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 59; Barros, II, vi, 5).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Albuquerque's troops stormed and held the bridge, immediately erecting barrel-and-timber barricades and bombarding the city and mosque for **nine to ten days** of grinding street fighting (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 25–26; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 59–60; Barros, II, vi, 5).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** *Sejarah Melayu* depicts Sultan Ahmad Shah riding into battle atop his war elephant (*Jiru* / *Jina*) accompanied by his religious teacher **Makhdum Sadar Jahan**, who fled the hail of Portuguese gunfire exclaiming it was "no place to study theology," while the elderly Bendahara Paduka Tuan had to be carried away in a litter protesting that he wished to die fighting (*Sejarah Melayu*, Brown trans. ch. 23).

#### 15–24 August 1511 — Flight of the Sultans, Final Sweep, and Sack of Melaka
- **`[Documented Fact]`** After days of bombardment and street-by-street clearance—with **15 August 1511** (Feast of the Assumption) marking the decisive capture of the mosque/town core in some accounts and **24 August 1511** (St. Bartholomew's Day) marking the final sweep and formal occupation of the city after **Sultan Mahmud Shah** and **Sultan Ahmad Shah** retreated inland toward Muar and Pagoh (see [Section 4.4](#44-dates-of-albuquerques-arrival-the-second-assault-and-the-fall-of-melaka))—Albuquerque ordered his troops to clear the remaining Malay resistance (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 26–28; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 60–61; Barros, II, vi, 6; Correia, Tomo II).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Albuquerque authorized a controlled sack: Portuguese soldiers and auxiliaries respected the safe-conduct banners of the Keling, Javanese, Pegu, and Chinese merchants who had submitted, while Malay quarters were thoroughly looted. Chroniclers report **2,000 to 3,000 artillery pieces** captured (about 2,000 of bronze, though many were small swivel guns/*lela*/*rentaka*) and Crown booty valued at over **200,000 cruzados** (excluding individual plunder and enslaved captives), plus 35 marks of gold and 25 of silver dug from buried caches (Castanheda, Book III, ch. 61; *Commentaries*, III, ch. 28; Barros, II, vi, 6).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** The Melakan war fleet of ~40 galleys manned by *Orang Laut* under the **Laksamana** was stationed near Singapore/Riau and arrived too late to relieve the capital; Portuguese chronicles claim the elderly Laksamana briefly opened talks with Albuquerque before Utimutiraja dissuaded him, after which the Laksamana consolidated his base at Singapore (Borschberg 2019, citing *Commentaries* and Portuguese chronicles).
- **`[Gameplay Hook]`** *Finale branching (blueprint Open Question #7):* Rather than a sudden binary Game Over, the siege plays out as a multi-day survival/evacuation arc where earlier port alliances, stockpiles, and intelligence determine which merchants survive with banners, whether royal archives/treasure escape to Pagoh, or whether the player transitions into serving the new regime (as Nina Chatu did) or fleeing with the court.

### Phase V — Immediate Aftermath for the Port and Trade (September 1511 – 1512+)

#### September – December 1511 — *A Famosa*, Governance, and Coinage
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Albuquerque immediately began constructing a stone fortress—initially a wooden stockade, then a masonry keep and walls called ***A Famosa*** (officially *Nossa Senhora da Assunção*)—beside the river mouth using forced labour (royal slaves) and stones quarried from the demolished Great Mosque and royal tombs (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 29, 32; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 62; Correia, Tomo II; Loureiro).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Albuquerque established a dual administration: Portuguese military/commercial leadership under **Rui de Brito Patalim** (first Captain of Melaka) and **Rui de Araújo** (first Factor), alongside co-opted Asian community heads—appointing **Nina Chatu** as **Bendahara** (head of the Keling/Hindu and non-Muslim communities) and **Utimutiraja** as head of the Javanese and Muslim inhabitants in Upeh (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 30–32; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 62–63; Thomaz 1985, 2000; Sar Desai 1969).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** At the urging of Nina Chatu and local merchants who complained of currency shortages after the sack, Albuquerque established a royal mint (c. November 1511), recalling and banning the Sultanate's tin *caixes* under pain of death, declaring local tin mines Crown property, and issuing new Portuguese coins: tin **dinheiros**, **soldos** (10 dinheiros), and **bastardos** (10 soldos), plus silver **malaqueses** and gold **católicos** (1,000 réis) (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 33; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 63; Correia, Tomo II; Da Cunha 1880).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** To restart commerce quickly, Albuquerque granted **customs-duty exemptions** to the first Keling ships arriving from Pulicat/Coromandel after August 1511 and sent diplomatic and commercial missions to **Siam** (Duarte Fernandes, then António de Miranda de Azevedo), **Pegu** (Rui Nunes da Cunha), **Java**, and the **Banda/Maluku Spice Islands** (**António de Abreu** and **Francisco Serrão**, sailing November 1511 with local pilots and a joint-venture junk co-owned with Nina Chatu) (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 34–38; Pires, Cortesão II, p. 281; Subrahmanyam 1986; Thomaz 1975, 1976).

#### 27 December 1511 – 1512 — Execution of Utimutiraja, Patih Kadir's Revolt, and Albuquerque's Departure
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Discovering that **Utimutiraja** was secretly corresponding with Sultan Mahmud's son (Sultan Ahmad / Prince Alauddin), monopolizing rice supplies, and seizing royal slaves, Albuquerque had Rui de Araújo read formal treason charges in public and **beheaded Utimutiraja** along with his son, son-in-law, and grandson on **27 December 1511**, rejecting his widow's offer of 100,000 cruzados in gold for their lives (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 36–37; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 68–69; Barros, II, vi, 7).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Albuquerque replaced Utimutiraja as head of the Javanese in Upeh with **Patih Kadir (Pate Quedir)**, who promptly rebelled in 1512 with Javanese followers and Sultan Mahmud's backing—disrupting Melaka's rice supply until defeated by Portuguese forces and followed by **Pati Unus** of Japara/Demak's massive Javanese naval assault on Melaka in **January 1513** (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 39; Pires, Cortesão II, pp. 281–283; Meilink-Roelofsz 1962).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Albuquerque sailed from Melaka for India in **December 1511 / January 1512** aboard the leaky flagship ***Flor de la Mar***, carrying the royal share of Melaka's plunder and diplomatic gifts from Siam. The ship wrecked off the coast of Sumatra (Pasai/Aru); Albuquerque survived on a raft, but almost the entire treasure was lost (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 43–44; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 74; Barros, II, vi, 8).

#### 1511–1515+ — Structural Impact on Melaka's Port and Regional Trade
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Sultan Mahmud retreated via Muar and Pagoh to **Pahang** and then **Bintan** (Riau Archipelago), where he maintained a rival Malay court and harassed Portuguese shipping (eventually founding the **Johor Sultanate** under his son Alauddin Riayat Shah II after 1528). *Sejarah Melayu* records that Mahmud later had his own son **Sultan Ahmad Shah** killed for mistreating senior nobles (*Sejarah Melayu*, Brown trans. ch. 23–24; Andaya & Andaya 2017; Borschberg 2019).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Sultan Mahmud also sent envoys (including **Tuan Haji**) to the **Ming court** in Beijing appealing for aid against the Portuguese; the Zhengde Emperor reprimanded the Portuguese and ordered Melaka's restoration, contributing to the Ming court's hostile reception of Tomé Pires's 1517–1521 embassy (*Ming Shilu*, Zhengde reign; Wade 2005; Andaya & Andaya 2017).
- **`[Scholarly Inference]`** Contrary to Tomé Pires's famous boast (*"Whoever is lord of Malacca has his hand on the throat of Venice"*, *Suma Oriental*, Cortesão II, p. 287), the Portuguese conquest **did not give Portugal a monopoly over Southeast Asian trade**. Instead, it **fragmented** the single entrepôt into a multipolar network: Gujarati, Arab, and Malay-Muslim merchants shifted their trade to **Aceh** (which boomed as a pepper-and-Red-Sea hub), **Johor/Bintan**, **Patani**, **Banten**, and **Brunei** (Meilink-Roelofsz 1962; Reid 1993; Subrahmanyam 1993; Pinto 2012).
- **`[Scholarly Inference]`** Within Melaka itself, trade recovered partially under a **Portuguese–Keling–Chinese–Pegu coalition** (centered on Coromandel textiles, tin, and spices), supplemented by Portuguese *cartaz* (pass) compulsion and customs duties (6–10%), though **Nina Chatu** himself committed suicide in **1514** after losing favor with Portuguese officials (Thomaz 1976, 1985, 2000; Subrahmanyam 1986; Pinto 2012).
- **`[Gameplay Hook]`** *Post-1511 epilogue / sandbox continuation:* If the game continues past August 1511 (blueprint Section 10), the port flips from a cosmopolitan Muslim-led entrepôt to a militarized fortress-port: Gujarati ships vanish (diverting to Aceh), rice supply from Java becomes perilous (Patih Kadir's revolt), new Portuguese tin coinage replaces old *caixes*, and the player must keep the harbour alive under Portuguese *feitor* oversight.

### Breakdown of Melaka's Four *Shahbandars*
- **`[Documented Fact]`** According to **Tomé Pires** (*Suma Oriental*, Book VI, Cortesão II, p. 265; see also Sar Desai 1969; Meilink-Roelofsz 1962), Melaka's four *shahbandars* were divided by regional trade networks:
  1. **Gujarati *Shahbandar*** — exclusively for ships from **Gujarat** (the single most important commercial bloc).
  2. **South Asian & North Sumatran *Shahbandar*** — for **Keling (Coromandel)**, **Bengal**, **Pegu**, and **Pasai/Pedir**.
  3. **Archipelagic *Shahbandar*** — for **Java**, **Maluku**, **Banda**, **Palembang**, **Borneo**, and **Luzon**.
  4. **East Asian & Mainland SE Asian *Shahbandar*** — for **China**, **Ryukyu (Lequios)**, and **Champa** (and Siam/eastern routes).
- **`[Documented Fact]`** Brás de Albuquerque's *Commentaries* (Part III, ch. 18, Birch trans. III, p. 96; quoted in Moreland 1920, p. 529) simplifies this list to four headmen representing **China, Java, Cambay (Gujarat), and Bengal**, all overseen by the **Temenggong** as chief customs judge.

## 3. What Happened to the Port and Trade Immediately After 1511

1. **Physical Transformation of the Harbour (`[Documented Fact]`):** The Malay royal palace and Great Mosque on the south bank were demolished to build the stone fortress ***A Famosa*** beside the river mouth, physically militarizing the waterfront and separating the walled Portuguese enclave (*Cidade*) from the Asian merchant suburbs (*Upeh* and *Hilir*) (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 29, 32; Correia, Tomo II; Loureiro).
2. **Monetary Overhaul (`[Documented Fact]`):** The Sultanate's tin *caixes* were outlawed under pain of death and replaced by Portuguese-minted tin (*dinheiros*, *soldos*, *bastardos*), silver (*malaqueses*), and gold (*católicos*) coins, while regional tin mines were claimed for the Portuguese Crown (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 33; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 63; Da Cunha 1880).
3. **Selective Merchant Retention & Customs Exemptions (`[Documented Fact]` / `[Scholarly Inference]`):** Albuquerque actively courted non-Muslim and cooperative Asian traders—exempting the first post-conquest Keling ships from customs duties, appointing Nina Chatu as Bendahara, and launching joint Crown–Keling trading voyages to Maluku, Pegu, and Pulicat (Subrahmanyam 1986; Thomaz 1976, 1985).
4. **Immediate Food & Security Crises (`[Documented Fact]`):** Because Melaka depended on imported Javanese and Pegu rice, Utimutiraja's alleged grain hoarding (Dec. 1511), Patih Kadir's revolt (1512), and Pati Unus's fleet attack (Jan. 1513) repeatedly threatened Portuguese Melaka with famine (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 36–39; Pires, Cortesão II, pp. 281–283; Meilink-Roelofsz 1962).
5. **Realignment of Regional Trade Routes (`[Scholarly Inference]`):** Muslim merchants (Gujaratis, Arabs, Persians, Bengalis, Malays) did not submit to Portuguese monopoly; they bypassed Melaka for **Aceh** (which captured the Red Sea pepper route), **Johor/Bintan** (Sultan Mahmud's exile court), **Patani**, **Banten**, and **Brunei**. Melaka remained a vital hub for the Estado da Índia's intra-Asian trade (especially Goa–Coromandel–Melaka–Macao), but it never again held the uncontested centrality it enjoyed before 1511 (Meilink-Roelofsz 1962; Reid 1993; Subrahmanyam 1993; Pinto 2012).

## 4. Where the Sources Disagree

Primary sources for 1509–1511 come from two sharply different traditions: **Portuguese letters and chronicles** (written to justify Crown policy, glorify Albuquerque, or settle factional scores between Almeida and Albuquerque partisans) and the **Malay court chronicle *Sejarah Melayu*** (surviving earliest in Raffles MS 18, c. 1612, written from a Johor court perspective to explain the moral and political causes of Melaka's fall). Modern historians highlight seven major areas of disagreement:

### 4.1 Dates of Sequeira's Arrival and the 1509 Ambush
- **Disagreement:** Most modern histories follow Castanheda and Barros in dating Sequeira's arrival at Melaka to **11 September 1509** (Castanheda, Book II, ch. 114; Abreu 1988), whereas some secondary summaries place his arrival in **August 1509** and the ambush anywhere from **late September to October 1509**.
- **`[Scholarly Inference]`** Exact day-by-day dating of the 1509 stay is reconstructed from chroniclers writing decades later (Castanheda 1551, Barros 1553, Correia c. 1560s); **11 September 1509** is the best-attested arrival date (Thomaz 2000; Pinto 2013).

### 4.2 Who Instigated and Who Betrayed the 1509 Ambush
- **Disagreement:**
  - **Castanheda, Barros, and *Commentaries*** blame **Gujarati and Muslim merchants** for bribing **Bendahara Tun Mutahir** (and convincing Sultan Mahmud) to ambush Sequeira, while crediting either a **Javanese woman** (lover of a Portuguese sailor in Utimutiraja's quarter) or **Chinese/Keling informants** with warning Sequeira in time (Castanheda, Book II, ch. 115–116; Barros, II, iv, 4; *Commentaries*, III, ch. 19).
  - **Tomé Pires** emphasizes Sultan Mahmud's own greed and hostility toward foreigners alongside Gujarati lobbying (Pires, Cortesão II, pp. 254–255).
  - ***Sejarah Melayu*** frames the 1509 episode differently: it notes popular alarm at the *Bengali putih* and the gold-chain etiquette faux pas with Tun Mutahir, and does not present the clash primarily as a Gujarati conspiracy (*Sejarah Melayu*, Brown trans. ch. 22–23).
- **`[Gameplay Hook]`** *Conflicting testimony (blueprint Section 06):* Never give the player one omniscient narrator—Gujarati, Keling, Javanese, and Malay court informants should give mutually contradictory accounts of why the 1509 violence erupted.

### 4.3 Why Bendahara Tun Mutahir Was Executed in 1510
- **Disagreement:**
  - ***Sejarah Melayu*** portrays Tun Mutahir's execution as a tragic miscarriage of justice driven by **Raja Mendeliar**'s and **Si Kitul**'s false treason accusation (abetted by **Laksamana Khoja Hassan**) plus Sultan Mahmud's personal grudge over **Tun Fatimah** (*Sejarah Melayu*, Brown trans. ch. 22).
  - **Portuguese sources** (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 19; Barros, II, vi, 1; Pires, Cortesão II, pp. 254–255) depict Tun Mutahir as genuinely overmighty, corrupt, and plotting to usurp the throne—or as a scapegoat whom Sultan Mahmud blamed for provoking the Portuguese in 1509.
- **`[Scholarly Inference]`** Historians read the purge as a real factional struggle between the entrenched Tamil-Muslim (*Jawi Pekan*) Bendahara clan and rival Malay/Indian court factions, dramatized in *Sejarah Melayu* as a moral parable against royal injustice (*derhaka* / tyranny) (Winstedt 1935; Cheah 1998; Andaya & Andaya 2017).

### 4.4 Dates of Albuquerque's Arrival, the Second Assault, and the Fall of Melaka
- **Disagreement:**
  - **Arrival off Melaka:** Most Portuguese sources give **1 July 1511** (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 20; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 53; Barros, II, vi, 2), though a few older summaries cite **mid-June 1511**.
  - **Second assault on the bridge:** Castanheda explicitly dates it to **Friday, 10 August 1511** (St. Lawrence's Day), whereas R. S. Whiteway (1899) gives **8 August 1511**.
  - **Fall / Conquest of Melaka:** Three different dates appear across sources and reference works:
    - **10 August 1511** — capture of the bridge (the tactical turning point).
    - **15 August 1511** (Feast of the Assumption) — capture of the main mosque/town center, commemorated in the fortress's formal name *Nossa Senhora da Assunção* (Birch in *Commentaries*, Vol. III; several European histories).
    - **24 August 1511** (St. Bartholomew's Day) — completion of the nine-to-ten-day street fighting, final sweep of Malay quarters, and definitive occupation after the Sultan's withdrawal (Castanheda, Book III, ch. 60–61; *Commentaries*, III, ch. 26–28; Subrahmanyam 1993).
- **`[Scholarly Inference]`** Rather than a single-day battle, the fall was a **two-week operational sequence (10–24 August 1511)**; different chroniclers chose the feast day that best suited their narrative climax (10 Aug. bridge, 15 Aug. Assumption/town core, 24 Aug. final clearance).

### 4.5 Force Sizes, Artillery Counts, and Casualties
- **Disagreement:**
  - **Portuguese force:** Albuquerque's own letters and *Commentaries* cite **18 ships** (Castanheda/Barros vary between **16 and 19 ships**), **~700–800 Portuguese soldiers**, and **200–300 Malabar/Indian auxiliaries** (~1,000–1,400 total including enslaved personnel) (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 15; Castanheda, Book III, ch. 50; Loureiro).
  - **Melakan defenders:** Rui de Araújo's 1510 letter estimated **~4,000 fighting men**; later Portuguese chronicles inflate total defenders to **20,000** (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 20) or **30,000–50,000** (Whiteway 1899), while Albuquerque himself reportedly noted that only ~4,000 were effective soldiers (*Cartas*).
  - **Artillery:** Castanheda reports **over 2,000 bronze guns** captured; *Commentaries* claims **3,000 guns** (~2,000 bronze).
  - **Portuguese casualties:** Portuguese chronicles report remarkably low battle deaths (~28–80 dead, though many wounded by poisoned arrows died later).
- **`[Scholarly Inference]`** Modern military historians note that "2,000–3,000 guns" included large numbers of light brass swivel guns (*lela* / *rentaka*) and matchlocks rather than heavy siege cannon, and that chroniclers inflated enemy troop numbers to magnify Albuquerque's victory (Reid 1993; Subrahmanyam 1993; Charney 2004).

### 4.6 Why Melaka Fell: Firearms vs. Internal Fragmentation
- **Disagreement:**
  - ***Sejarah Melayu*** dramatizes Portuguese gunfire as shocking and overwhelming—depicting defenders asking "What is this round thing that kills us?"—while simultaneously framing the defeat as the moral consequence of Sultan Mahmud's execution of Bendahara Tun Mutahir and court decay (*Sejarah Melayu*, Brown trans. ch. 22–23).
  - **Portuguese chronicles** emphasize European tactical discipline, naval firepower, the fortified junk at the bridge, and the numerical/technological inadequacy of Melakan gunners (Araújo's 1510 letter; *Commentaries*, III, ch. 22–26).
- **`[Scholarly Inference]`** Modern scholars reject the myth that Melaka lacked firearms—as the 2,000+ captured guns prove—and instead attribute the fall to a **convergence of four factors**: (1) Portuguese superior heavy shipborne artillery and disciplined infantry assault on the bridge chokepoint; (2) the **1510 court purge** and leadership split between Mahmud, Ahmad, and the infirm Bendahara Paduka Tuan; (3) the **absence of the Laksamana's fleet** at Singapore; and (4) the **neutrality/defection of Melaka's foreign merchant communities** (Keling, Javanese, Chinese, Pegu) who had little incentive to die for Sultan Mahmud (Meilink-Roelofsz 1962; Reid 1993; Andaya & Andaya 2017; Borschberg 2019; Pinto 2013).

### 4.7 Utimutiraja's Role: Opportunistic Broker or "Biggest Traitor"?
- **Disagreement:**
  - **Portuguese sources** portray Utimutiraja as a pragmatic, double-dealing Javanese magnate who negotiated neutrality in August 1511 to protect his Upeh quarter, accepted Portuguese office, and then secretly conspired with Sultan Mahmud's son—justifying Albuquerque's December 1511 execution (*Commentaries*, III, ch. 24, 36–37; Barros, II, vi, 5–7).
  - **Later Malay historiography** often casts Utimutiraja (alongside Nina Chatu) as an archetypal foreign traitor who sold out Melaka.
- **`[Scholarly Inference]`** Historians of maritime Southeast Asia view Utimutiraja neither as a simple villain nor a loyalist, but as an autonomous **diasporic merchant-lord** (*orang kaya*) operating in a port where foreign quarters functioned almost like self-governing consulates; his primary loyalty was to his Javanese commercial network and rice trade, not to either the Malay Sultan or the Portuguese Crown (Meilink-Roelofsz 1962; Moreland 1920; Reid 1993).

## 5. Summary of Gameplay Hooks for *Port Master: Melaka 1511*

Mapping the 1509–1511 arc directly onto [`docs/gameplay-blueprint.md`](../gameplay-blueprint.md) (Sections 06, 09, 10, and Open Question #7):

1. **Three-Act Historical Arc (`[Gameplay Hook]`):**
   - **Act I — Boom & First Contact (early–late 1509):** High traffic, four *shahbandar* blocs competing for berths, rumors from Pedir/Pasai, Sequeira's disruptive arrival (11 Sept. 1509), and the ambush/factory-seizure crisis.
   - **Act II — Captives, Espionage & Court Purge (1510):** Smuggled intelligence hidden inside Detailed Inspection cargos (Nina Chatu / Rui de Araújo channel), the sudden **1510 execution of Bendahara Tun Mutahir**, administrative drift under the infirm **Bendahara Paduka Tuan**, and factional polarization between Gujaratis, Kelings, and Javanese.

2. **Answering Blueprint Open Question #7 (*Fixed, Alterable, or Branching 1511?*) (`[Gameplay Hook]`):** The historical record naturally supports **consequential branching without trivializing history**: even if Portuguese naval power makes a siege unavoidable, the player's 1509–1510 decisions on rice reserves, merchant trust (Keling/Javanese/Chinese), intelligence leaks, and court stability can meaningfully determine *who defects*, *whether the port starves*, *whether the Laksamana arrives in time*, and *which community survives the sack*.

Brown, *Sejarah Melayu or 'Malay Annals'*, *Journal of the Malayan Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society* (JMBRAS) 25.2–3 (1952), ch. 22–24; also W. G. Shellabear recension. *(Malay court literary-historical chronicle; omits calendar dates and moralizes royal conduct, but indispensable for internal Malay court politics, Tun Mutahir's fall, Bendahara Paduka Tuan, and Sultan Ahmad.)*
- ***Ming Shilu* (Veritable Records of the Ming Dynasty).** Zhengde reign entries on Melaka's post-1511 envoys (trans. Geoff Wade, *Southeast Asia in the Ming Shi-lu*, ARI/NUS, 2005).
