# Monsoon Calendar of Melaka Shipping (c. 1511)

## Scope and evidence

- `[Documented fact]` Tomé Pires called Melaka “the end of monsoons and the beginning of others” and described ships sailing in “regular monsoons” (*Suma Oriental*, II, pp. 284, 286). He gives several route schedules, not a single calendar of calls. See [Commodities §2.1](commodities.md#21-the-monsoon-engine) for the broad wind system and commodity flows; the route dates below refine that overview. See [1511 Events](1511-events.md) for the conquest chronology.
- `[Scholarly inference]` This is a reconstruction of trade **around** 1511, not a log of 1511. Pires wrote after the conquest and mixes remembered practice with routes still running or reopening (II, pp. 278, 282–283). “Arrive,” “leave,” and “stay” below refer to **Melaka**, unless another port is named. A sailing window at the far end is not an arrival date, and a trading season is not a measure of time alongside a berth.

`[Documented fact]` marks what a primary source records or prescribes, not independent proof of how often it happened. `[Scholarly inference]` marks a published reconstruction, an estimate from the cited evidence, or an explicit gap in that evidence. `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` marks a design choice, not a historical assertion.

## Routes

### 1. Gujarat and the Red Sea

- `[Documented fact]` Pires says merchants from Cairo, Mecca and Aden could not reach Melaka “in a single monsoon” in the trade he describes: Cairo goods went via Tor, Jidda and Aden to Gujarat, where merchants joined Gujarati ventures. “From there they embark in March and sail direct for Malacca; and on the return journey they call at the Maidive Islands” (II, pp. 269–270). Pires gives March as **departure from Gujarat**, not arrival at Melaka.
- `[Scholarly inference]` **Arrival:** April–May is a working estimate for the March Cambay sailing, not a port record. **Departure and stay:** Meilink-Roelofsz reconstructs a short call (weeks rather than a season) for ships aiming to be **back on India's west coast by late May**; that implies an April or early-May departure from Melaka on that itinerary, not a late-May departure deadline there (pp. 37–38, 64). The precise duration and what share of ships made this quick turnaround are unknown.
- `[Scholarly inference]` **Other sailings were possible.** Beaujard reads al-Mahrī's Gujarat departure window as roughly late March–late April, with an early-April optimum and a second opportunity around September. The latter makes a later Melaka call, roughly October, possible but does not establish a regular second wave or its stay (Beaujard, pp. 11–12).
- `[Scholarly inference]` **Direct Red Sea option:** Arabic pilot directions include direct Arabian sailings in both directions. Approximate converted sailing dates include an August **departure from Aden** (hence a possible autumn arrival in Melaka) and late December to mid-February **departure from Melaka** toward Aden. These are navigational recommendations from different pilots, not paired calls by the same ship; neither the regularity of a direct fleet nor its typical Melaka stay is established (Peacock, section on Ibn Mājid and al-Mahrī; Abu-Lughod, pp. 256–257, as adapted in the UC Davis sailing-date table).
- `[Documented fact]` Gujarati ships were also present in Melaka at Albuquerque's early-July 1511 arrival (Pires II, pp. 278–279). This is an observation in a port preparing for war, not a date for their arrival or proof that all Gujarati ships stayed until July.

### 2. Coromandel and Bengal

- `[Documented fact]` **Coromandel/Pulicat:** “They leave here in January and come back in October. They take a month to go and another to come back”; Pires adds that Malacca junks sometimes sailed to Pulicat (II, p. 272). **Arrival October; departure January** in his description.
- `[Scholarly inference]` **Coromandel stay:** about three months on the October–January cycle, not a measured average. Meilink-Roelofsz reads these months as the Kling merchants' trading season in Melaka (pp. 67–68).
- `[Documented fact]` **Bengal:** “They leave here at the beginning of August and they reach Bengal in thirty days; they stay there trading; they leave there on the first of February and they take as long again to Malacca” (Pires I, p. 93). Pires calls the Bengal and Pulicat voyages annual (II, p. 284).
- `[Scholarly inference]` **Bengal arrival:** around early March from the February departure and thirty-day passage. **Departure:** early August. **Stay:** about five months *if* the same vessel followed both legs without an intervening trip. The quoted “stay there trading” refers to Bengal, not Melaka. Neither this cycle nor the October Coromandel arrival fits a uniform May–September arrival rule for Indian shipping (Pires I, p. 93; II, pp. 271–272; Mukherjee, on East Indian Ocean winds and Bengal).
- `[Documented fact]` Rui de Araújo's 6 February 1510 letter instead gives **April arrival and September departure** for Bengal (in *Cartas*, III, p. 10). `[Scholarly inference]` That also implies about five months if the same ships made both calls, but a later spring arrival and later clearing of the port than in Pires.

### 3. Pegu

- `[Documented fact]` **Arrival:** Pires describes vessels leaving Pegu in February and arriving “at the end of March and during the whole of April,” on a circuit serving Melaka, Pasai and Pedir. **Departure:** “They leave here on the first of July and go to Pase to load up with pepper and in August they go to Martaban.” July is the Melaka exit; August is the leg out of Pasai (Pires I, pp. 98–99).
- `[Scholarly inference]` **Stay:** about two to three months in Melaka for vessels following those dates. This does not mean every ship returned: Meilink-Roelofsz notes that junks could be sold in Melaka (pp. 69–70). Pires' separate “eight or nine months on the journeys” under Siam and Pegu refers to voyages in a discussion of investment, not a Melaka layover (II, p. 284).
- `[Documented fact]` Araújo says Pegu ships come and go at the same time as the Bengali ships in his letter, **April and September** (*Cartas*, III, p. 10). `[Scholarly inference]` That would be about five months rather than Pires' two to three; April arrivals have better support than any single departure month.

### 4. Sumatra

- `[Documented fact]` Pires describes small cargo craft associated with Pasai and Pedir and reports that ten or twelve Palembang junks **would come annually** to Melaka (I, pp. 145, 155–156). He mentions Pedir rice craft sailing “with the first winds” without naming a month in that passage (I, p. 139). His July Pasai call belongs to the Pegu return itinerary (I, p. 99), not to all Sumatran ships.
- `[Scholarly inference]` **Arrival and departure:** no common monthly window is established; the shorter Strait routes can plausibly supply scattered arrivals and departures in both seasons, with individual ports still subject to winds, trade interruptions and river access. **Stay:** unknown; a shorter crossing does not establish a shorter stay. Keep Pasai/Pedir port calls on long-distance voyages separate from local Sumatran arrivals (Pires I, pp. 139, 145, 155–156; Meilink-Roelofsz, pp. 37–38).

### 5. Java and the Moluccas

- `[Documented fact]` **Java:** “The voyage of Java is made at the beginning of January, in the first monsoon, and they come back from May onwards, up to August or September of the same year” (Pires II, p. 284). This is a Melaka-based round trip, giving **January departures and May–September returns**, not a record of the schedules of all Java-based crews.
- `[Scholarly inference]` **Java-based arrivals:** May–September with southeasterly winds. **Departure:** generally January on northwesterlies, sometimes as early as late November or December. **Stay:** potentially several months (about four to eight if a May–September arrival waits until January), not a fleet average (Meilink-Roelofsz, p. 38).
- `[Documented fact]` Araújo puts **Java arrivals in October–November** and says these vessels went on to Pedir (*Cartas*, III, pp. 9–10). `[Scholarly inference]` Pires dates the return of a Melaka-based venture, while Araújo describes incoming vessels on a different itinerary. Do not assume the latter stayed until January or that all Java calls stopped in September.
- `[Documented fact]` **Moluccas/Banda:** Pires describes a merchant itinerary from Melaka via Java and Bima/Sumbawa to Banda and the Moluccas, trading at stops, and distinguishes a route via Borneo and Buton (I, pp. 219–220). Birch's *Commentaries* records António de Abreu's expedition leaving Melaka in **November 1511**; Cortesão cites sources placing it in December and reports Abreu's return in **December 1512** (*Commentaries*, III, p. 164; Cortesão, I, introduction, “The Voyage of Discovery to the Spice Islands”).
- `[Scholarly inference]` **Moluccas-linked calls:** a late-year departure is consistent with the outbound eastern-archipelago window. May–September is a reasonable window for **cargo reaching Melaka via Java**, not a documented return schedule for direct Moluccan hulls. A multi-stop expedition could miss that window, as Abreu's December return illustrates. **Stay:** unknown for Moluccan vessels; the dates above describe an expedition's time away, not its layover in Melaka (Pires I, pp. 219–220; II, p. 284; Meilink-Roelofsz, p. 38).

### 6. China and Ryukyu

- `[Scholarly inference]` **China arrival:** chiefly late in the old year and early in the new one (roughly November–February), with the northeast winds. **Departure:** around late June into early summer on the return winds. **Stay:** potentially four to seven months between those windows if remaining in Melaka, but Meilink-Roelofsz also notes that early arrivals could trade onward to Java rather than spend the whole interval in port (p. 38).
- `[Documented fact]` Pires says ships going **from Melaka to China** “leave here at the end of June for a good voyage” (I, p. 123). The *Commentaries* reports five Chinese junks already in port when Albuquerque arrived in early July 1511, detained by the sultan for several days. Before the second assault, their captains asked to go because “the season of their monsoon had arrived”; Albuquerque gave permission and asked them to go by Siam with an envoy (*Commentaries*, III, chs. XXI, XXV–XXVI; Pires II, pp. 278–279). The episode puts them in port in July with a departure in the July–early August siege sequence, not on a known arrival date or a firm annual June cutoff.
- `[Documented fact]` Araújo's 1510 letter gives **April arrivals and May departures** for Chinese junks in its surviving text; the printed passage has gaps nearby (*Cartas*, III, p. 9). `[Scholarly inference]` This implies a shorter call of roughly a month for that schedule, not a general China dwell time. The winter-in/early-summer-out reconstruction does not cover every report.
- `[Documented fact]` **Ryukyu:** Pires reports one to three *Lequeos* junks annually, but the printed translation reads “They leave here in [blank]” (I, p. 130). A surviving Ryukyuan credential for a Melaka-bound mission is dated **2 September 1509** (*Rekidai Hōan*, vol. 42, doc. 3, trans. Sakamaki, p. 386). That is an issue date in Ryukyu, not a Melaka arrival or departure.
- `[Documented fact]` Araújo gives the *Gores* **January arrival, April departure and about forty days at sea each way** (*Cartas*, III, p. 9). Birch's *Commentaries* instead says they “set out for Malaca in the month of January, and begin their return journey in August and September” (III, pp. 89–90). The latter makes January an origin departure, not an arrival; the two accounts should not be averaged.
- `[Scholarly inference]` **Ryukyu stay:** about three months on Araújo's schedule if the *Gores* are identified with Ryukyu, as Nakajima and Cortesão do. Nakajima suggests the 1509 mission may be the one Araújo describes; this is not a confirmed match. There is no secure average or uniform window, and the Chinese junks of July 1511 are a separate observation (Nakajima, §§1, 2, 6; Cortesão, I, note to the *Liu Kiu* section).

### 7. Siam

- `[Documented fact]` Pires says up to thirty junks a year **used to** come from Siam and reports a twenty-two-year break in trade after a political quarrel (I, pp. 107–109). Yet his Melaka account lists Siamese among the trading peoples with the caveat that some listed places were visited from Melaka rather than sending their own ships (II, pp. 268–269). The *Commentaries* records a laden junk that had just left Melaka for Siam as the Portuguese approached in early July 1511, as well as the later Chinese-junk envoy passage (III, p. 65 and ch. XXV).
- `[Scholarly inference]` **Arrival:** northeast-wind months (roughly November–February) are a plausible southbound Gulf of Siam window **if** direct trade operates, not a demonstrated 1511 wave. **Departure:** summer return winds are plausible on the Gulf route, with an early-July 1511 Siam-bound voyage actually reported. **Stay:** unknown; the records give no paired dates. Neither Siam-bound vessel proves a regular Siamese-flagged fleet, and Tenasserim's Indian Ocean traffic should not inherit a Gulf calendar (Pires I, pp. 107–109; Meilink-Roelofsz, pp. 72–73 and note discussing *Commentaries*, III, p. 65; Beaujard, “Wind Patterns”).

- `[Scholarly inference]` **Busiest:** Meilink-Roelofsz proposes **December–March** for greatest *market activity* (p. 38), not peak hull count. Coromandel, China and still-present Java cargoes overlap around December–January, and Bengal and Pegu start arriving in March. **Late March–April** is another credible time for arrival or unloading pressure, when Bengal and Pegu overlap with ships still trading from China and some Gujarati calls. The evidence cannot rank those periods by occupied berths.
- `[Scholarly inference]` **Quiet, not empty:** September is a candidate for fewer *new long-distance arrivals* before the Coromandel and main China seasons. That is not a quiet anchorage guarantee: Araújo still places Bengal and Pegu departures there, and late Java and local traffic could be active. Meilink-Roelofsz describes regional trade sustaining the market outside the peak and post-monsoon supply gaps that could raise prices (pp. 38, 50). The sources used here do not establish any month when the port emptied.

## Where the sources disagree or run out

- `[Scholarly inference]` **One wind label cannot stand in for all western routes.** The broad May–September western-arrival label in [Commodities §2.1](commodities.md#21-the-monsoon-engine) is not a safe scheduler for Pires' October Coromandel, early-March Bengal or late-March–April Pegu schedules. For Gujarat, the March departure in Pires and al-Mahrī's early-April optimum as interpreted by Beaujard need not conflict: one describes a trading practice, the other recommends sailing conditions. Meilink-Roelofsz associates the regular early Gujarat passage with northeast winds; Beaujard treats the April opening as the head of the southwest monsoon. Use the route-level dates over a universal wind label (Pires I, pp. 93, 98–99; II, pp. 269–272; Meilink-Roelofsz, pp. 37–38; Beaujard, pp. 10–12).
- `[Scholarly inference]` **Practice and capability differ.** Pires' Gujarat relay sits alongside the direct Arabian navigation in the pilot literature. The July Gujarati presence and July–early August Chinese departure record limit how literally a reconstructed early Gujarat turnaround or late-June Chinese deadline can be read. A siege observation establishes presence or an opportunity to sail, not a normal commercial schedule (Pires II, pp. 269–270, 278–279; *Commentaries*, III, chs. XXI, XXV–XXVI; Peacock).
- `[Scholarly inference]` **The chronologies are not interchangeable.** Relative to Pires, Araújo places Bengal later on both legs and Pegu later on departure; his Chinese and Java dates also differ from the baseline. For Ryukyu, Araújo and the later chronicle disagree about what happened in January and when ships left Melaka. These texts may capture different fleets, years or legs; do not choose an exact day for spawning ships on that basis. The November/December Abreu discrepancy also concerns one expedition, not ordinary departures (*Cartas*, III, pp. 9–10; Pires I, pp. 93, 98–99, 123, 130; II, p. 284; Cortesão, I, introduction; *Commentaries*, III, pp. 89–90, 164).

## Suggested gameplay abstractions

- `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` **Stage C — Living Economy:** Give each route separate arrival weights and preferred departure windows. Start with the better-attested Coromandel, Bengal, Pegu, Java and China patterns rather than one SW/NE toggle. Let ships clear their berths after unloading even if their crews keep trading and the hull waits at anchor; use flexible Sumatran calls to sustain a baseline. Allow inventory to bridge the mismatch between buying and selling seasons. Model a lull with fewer new deep-sea arrivals, not a forced empty port.
- `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` **Stage D — Narrative and Political Layer:** Treat the Siam dispute and the 1511 detention of Chinese crews as event-driven exceptions to ordinary sailings. Show the difference between “wind not yet favourable” and “captain ready, clearance withheld” in the daily report. Give a Ryukyu mission its own rare event instead of cloning Chinese call frequency or dwell time.
