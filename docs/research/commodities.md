## 1. Executive Summary & Blueprint Comparison

`docs/gameplay-blueprint.md` (Section 07) groups the 13 initial commodities into five playability-oriented source regions (Java, China, India, Middle East, and Melaka/surrounding region). Historical evidence from c. 1511 shows that Melaka functioned primarily as a **transshipment entrepôt** rather than a major producer—Tomé Pires estimated total annual trade at roughly 2.4 million *cruzados*, of which only tin (and minor forest products) originated in Melaka's own territories `[Documented fact]` (Pires 1944; Reid 1989).

Several blueprint groupings are reasonable simplifications of **who carried** the goods (e.g., Javanese ships bringing Banda nutmeg and Timor sandalwood), while two major divergences stand out:
1. **Pepper** arriving at Melaka came heavily from **northern/eastern Sumatra** (Pedir, Pasai, Kampar, Indragiri), **Kedah**, and **Sunda (West Java)**—not solely from Malabar/India `[Documented fact]` (Pires 1944; Fitriansyach & Wijaya 2024).
2. **Iron** was scarce across maritime Southeast Asia and reached Melaka via **multiple regional and long-distance circuits** (Karimata/Borneo, China, Siam, and India), rather than India alone `[Scholarly inference]` (Reid 2011; Pires 1944).

## 2. Cross-Cutting Foundations of Melaka's Trade (c. 1511)

### 2.1 The Monsoon Engine
- `[Documented fact]` Tomé Pires famously described Melaka as *"a city made for merchandise more than any other in the world, the end of monsoons and the beginning of others"* (Pires 1944, Vol. II; Villiers 1988). Because ocean-going sailing vessels could not complete a round trip between the western Indian Ocean and China in a single wind season, Melaka served as the natural midway depot where cargoes were stored in stone/semi-submerged warehouses (*gudang*) between monsoons (Pires 1944; Borschberg 2019).
- `[Documented fact]` **Southwest Monsoon (roughly April/May–September):** Carried ships eastward from the Red Sea/Aden, Hormuz, Gujarat, Malabar, Coromandel, Bengal, and Pegu toward the Strait of Melaka. The 16th-century Arab pilot Sulaymān al-Mahrī records the optimal departure from Gujarat for Melaka around the 140th day after *Nairūz* (roughly **mid-April**), and from Malabar around the 160th day (early May), riding the "head of the wind" before peak SW storms closed western Indian harbors (Tibbetts 1971; Beaujard 2019).
- `[Documented fact]` **Northeast Monsoon (roughly November–March/April):** Carried junks southward from China (Guangdong/Fujian), Ryukyu, Champa, Siam, and Borneo to Melaka, while simultaneously carrying departing ships westward from Melaka back to India, Aden, and Hormuz (Pires 1944; Meilink-Roelofsz 1962).
- `[Scholarly inference]` **Archipelago Winds (Java, Banda, Timor, Moluccas):** Vessels sailing between Melaka and the eastern archipelago depended on alternating West/NW winds (roughly Nov–Mar) to sail *east* from Melaka to Java/Banda/Moluccas, and East/SE winds (roughly May–Sep) to return *west* to Melaka laden with nutmeg, cloves, and sandalwood (Pires 1944; Villiers 1990).
- `[Documented fact]` **Strait & Coastal Traffic:** Short-haul vessels from east Sumatra (Pedir, Pasai, Kampar, Indragiri) and the Malay tin districts (Selangor, Klang, Bruas, Perak) could reach Melaka across much of the year in the sheltered waters of the Strait (Vann 2014; Pires 1944).
- `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` Model the campaign calendar in **two primary monsoon seasons** (West/SW Arrivals vs. East/NE Arrivals) plus year-round regional coastal traffic. Missing a ship's departure window before the monsoon reverses should carry heavy diplomatic and economic consequences, explaining why waiting ships cannot linger indefinitely at anchor.

### 2.2 The Four *Syahbandars* (Harbour Masters)
- `[Documented fact]` Melaka organized foreign commerce under **four *Syahbandars*** (*Xabandares*), each supervising a distinct regional grouping, acting as custodian of weights/measures, collecting dues, and arbitrating disputes (Pires 1944, Vol. II; Borschberg 2019):
  1. **Gujaratis** (explicitly noted by Pires as *"the most important"*).
  2. **Coromandel (Keling), Bengal, Pegu, and Pasai** merchants.
  3. **Java, Moluccas, Banda, Palembang, Tanjungpura (Borneo), and Luzon (*Lugoes*)** merchants.
  4. **China, Ryukyu (*Lequjos*), Zhangzhou (*Chancheo*), and Champa** merchants.
- `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` Even if the player acts as a unified port administrator (see sibling research on the port official), the four *Syahbandar* constituencies provide a historically grounded faction framework for merchant trust, rumors, and berthing politics.

### 2.3 Weights, Measures, Currency, and Customs
- `[Documented fact]` **The *Dacing* (Steelyard) and Two *Bahars*:** Goods were weighed on an official Chinese-style beam balance (*dacing* / Portuguese *dachem*). António Nunes' *Livro dos Pesos da India* (1554, fol. 31) and Tomé Pires record **two distinct Melaka *bahars***, each of 200 *kati* (*cates*), depending on the commodity (Nunes 1554; Pires 1944; Pinto 2012):
  - **Large *bahar* (*baar grande*):** ~**210.3 kg** (1 *kati* ≈ 1.051 kg). Used for **pepper, cloves, nutmeg, mace, sandalwood, frankincense/incense, iron, brazilwood, sulfur, saltpeter, and myrrh**.
  - **Small *bahar* (*baar pequeno*):** ~**183.6 kg** (1 *kati* ≈ 0.918 kg). Used for **tin (*calaim*), Chinese silk, ivory, opium, rosewater, and camphor**.
  - **Precious *kati*:** ~**803.25 g** (20 *tahil* of ~40.16–41.23 g), used for **gold, musk, seed-pearls, coral, and agarwood (*kalambak*)** (Nunes 1554; Pinto 2012).
- `[Documented fact]` **Weighing Fee:** Weighing was mandatory—*"everything was weighed, even tar-lamps"*—at a fee of **1 *calaim* per 100** (Pires 1944).
- `[Documented fact]` **Currency:** Local small coinage consisted of tin ***cashes*** stamped with the ruler's name (coins of earlier sultans remained current). **100 cashes = 1 *calaim*** (~33 oz of tin); **100 *calains* = 3 *cruzados*** (Portuguese gold accounting unit used by Pires). Gold and silver circulated **by weight**, not as locally minted Melaka gold coins (Pires 1944).
- `[Documented fact]` **Customs & Valuation:** Foreign ships from the west (Gujarat, India, Arabia, Persia, Pegu, Bengal) paid **6% ad valorem** (resident merchants paid **3%**), plus customary presents to the Sultan, Bendahara, Temenggung, Laksamana, and Syahbandar (e.g., 1 cloth per 100). To avoid extortion, Gujarati merchants routinely requested lump-sum ship assessment by a panel of valuers (a Gujarati ship was valued at a minimum of 7 *cates* of tin / ~21,000 *cruzados*) (Pires 1944, Vol. II; Barbosa 1918). Eastern vessels (China, Java, Moluccas, Siam) generally gave customary presents rather than a flat 6% cash tax, and provisions/foodstuffs were taxed lightly or via gifts to keep the city fed (Pires 1944).
- `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` The dual *bahar* system (~210 kg vs. ~184 kg) is an ideal **Detailed Inspection** hook: a dishonest captain declaring spices on the small *dacing*—or silk/tin on the wrong *kati* standard—produces a subtle weight discrepancy the player can catch using the reference manual.

## 3. Commodity-by-Commodity Profiles

### 1. Tin
- **Blueprint Network vs. History:** Blueprint lists *Melaka and surrounding region*. This is historically accurate—tin was Melaka's single most important local export `[Documented fact]` (Pires 1944; Ma Huan 1970).
- **Origins:** `[Documented fact]` Alluvial tin deposits along the western Malay Peninsula subject to Melaka: **Selangor, Klang, Bernam, Sungai Jugra, Manjung (*Mimjam*), Bruas, Perak**, and **Kedah** (Pires 1944, Vol. II; D. G. E. Hall 1981). Siam also exported tin to Melaka (Pires 1944, Vol. I: 108).
- **Merchant Communities & Routes:** `[Documented fact]` Malay riverine chiefs sent tin to Melaka both as **royal tribute** and as commercial cargo on small coastal vessels. From Melaka, tin was re-exported westward by **Gujarati, Keling, and Bengali** ships to India and the Middle East, and eastward by **Chinese** junks (Pires 1944; Mukherjee 2020).
- **Monsoon Season:** `[Scholarly inference]` Arrived **year-round** via sheltered river and coastal craft along the Strait of Melaka, making it a dependable local buffer when ocean monsoons were slow.
- **Measurement & Pricing:** `[Documented fact]` Ma Huan (1433) describes tin ore washed in sieves, smelted into standard blocks, and tied **40 blocks to a bundle**, serving directly as trade currency (Ma Huan 1970). Pires records bulk tin weighed on the **small *bahar*** (~183.6 kg), while tin *calains* served as money: **100 *calains* = 3 *cruzados*** (roughly 1 *cruzado* per ~33.3 *calains* / ~69 lbs in Cortesão's calculation) (Pires 1944; Nunes 1554).
- **Gameplay Notes:** `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` Tin bridges **commodity and liquidity**—shortages disrupt both export trade and local small change. Inspection hooks: short-weight ingots, lead-adulterated tin blocks, or tribute shipments misdeclared as private merchant lots to dodge dues.
- **Sources:** Tomé Pires, *Suma Oriental* (Cortesão ed., 1944, Vols. I–II); Ma Huan, *Yingya Shenglan* (Mills ed., 1970, Melaka section); António Nunes, *Livro dos Pesos da India* (1554, fol. 31); D. G. E. Hall (1981), *A History of South-East Asia*.

### 2. Rice
- **Blueprint Network vs. History:** Blueprint lists *Java*. Java was indeed a premier supplier, but Melaka's survival depended on **multiple rice baskets**: **Java, Siam, Pegu (Burma), Bengal, and Sumatra** `[Documented fact]` (Pires 1944; Borschberg 2019).
- **Origins:** `[Documented fact]` Melaka's own hinterland produced almost no rice, forcing massive food imports (Pires 1944; Ma Huan 1970). Major suppliers were **Java** (northern *pasisir* ports: Tuban, Gresik, Demak—up to ~20,000 *bahars*/year noted in Portuguese accounts), **Siam** (up to 30 junks/year bringing rice, salted fish, and *arak*, estimated at >10,000 tons around 1500), **Pegu** (several thousand tons/year), **Bengal**, and **Sumatra** (Pasai/Pedir) (Pires 1944, Vol. I: 108; Tarafdar 2019).
- **Merchant Communities & Routes:** `[Documented fact]` Carried by **Javanese** (Syahbandar #3), **Siamese** (Syahbandar #4), **Peguan**, **Bengali**, and **Pasai** (Syahbandar #2) merchants. Rice was the high-tonnage ballast and provisioning backbone of the port; Pires remarked that in Melaka *"they prize garlic and onions more than musk, benzoin, and other precious things"*—evidence of an urban food-deficit market (Pires 1944).
- **Monsoon Season:** `[Scholarly inference]` **Staggered across both monsoons**, which kept Melaka fed: **Pegu and Bengal** rice arrived on the SW monsoon (roughly May–Sep); **Javanese** rice on the E/SE monsoon (roughly May–Sep); **Siamese** junks on the NE monsoon (roughly Nov–Mar) (Pires 1944; Meilink-Roelofsz 1962).
- **Measurement & Pricing:** `[Documented fact]` Measured by volume in ***gantang*** (standard Malay volumetric measure) and traded in bulk junk loads (Borschberg 2015). Foodstuffs received preferential customs treatment (presents rather than full 6% duties) to encourage steady supply (Pires 1944). `[Scholarly inference]` Exact 1511 per-*gantang* market prices fluctuated sharply with monsoon delays and military blockades; no single fixed 1511 price is preserved in Pires.
- **Gameplay Notes:** `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` Rice is the game's **civic survival resource** (matching the blueprint's Day 1 scenario). Giving rice junks berthing priority prevents famine/unrest, while Royal First Purchase lets the state build emergency granary reserves before the 1511 siege. Inspection hooks: damp/moldy sacks at the bottom of a hold, husks/chaff mixed in to pad volume, or contraband spices hidden under duty-exempt rice.
- **Sources:** Tomé Pires, *Suma Oriental* (1944, Vol. I: 108 & Vol. II); Ma Huan, *Yingya Shenglan* (1970); Peter Borschberg (2015, 2019); Momtazur Rahman Tarafdar (2019); M. A. P. Meilink-Roelofsz (1962).

### 3. Timber
- **Blueprint Network vs. History:** Blueprint lists *Java*. Historically, **Java** (teak forests of Rembang/north coast) and **Pegu (Burma)** were the two great maritime timber and shipbuilding suppliers, supplemented by **Sumatra** and the **Malay Peninsula** `[Documented fact]` (Pires 1944; Manguin 1980).
- **Origins:** `[Documented fact]` High-grade **teak** (*Tectona grandis*) for ocean-going *jong* (junks) came from **Pegu** and **northern Java**; local tropical hardwoods, masts, and resin/dammar came from the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra. Melaka itself lacked capacity to build large ocean-going *jong* (though it built light war-craft like *lancharas* and *calaluzes*) (Pires 1944; Manguin 1980).
- **Merchant Communities & Routes:** `[Documented fact]` **Peguan** and **Javanese** merchants (Syahbandars #2 and #3) brought both raw timber/masts and **completed ships as merchandise**: Pires notes that Javanese and Melaka merchants commissioned junks in Pegu, where Peguan crews *"bring the merchandise and the junks all as merchandise, and having sold the merchandise they used to sell the junks"* (Pires 1944, Vol. I: 195).
- **Monsoon Season:** `[Scholarly inference]` **Pegu** timber/junks arrived on the **SW monsoon** (May–Sep); **Javanese** timber on the **E/SE monsoon** (May–Sep); local Malay/Sumatran timber arrived year-round.
- **Measurement & Pricing:** `[Scholarly inference]` Unlike weighed spices, structural timber and shipbuilding stock were assessed by **piece, plank, mast, or finished hull** rather than *bahar* (aromatic woods like sandalwood and dyewoods like brazilwood/sappanwood were weighed by the large *bahar*). Specific 1511 Melaka plank prices are not recorded in Pires.
- **Gameplay Notes:** `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` Timber is an **infrastructure and fleet-maintenance input** (expanding berths, repairing wharves/warehouses, refitting ships). Note the dramatic 1511–1512 historical shock: after Albuquerque burned the Javanese/Malay junks, Pires observed that Gresik merchants could no longer sail because Pegu junk sales had halted for five years (Pires 1944: 195; Brown 2007). Inspection hooks: green/unseasoned wood sold as seasoned teak, shipworm-damaged planks, or hollow-log smuggling.
- **Sources:** Tomé Pires, *Suma Oriental* (1944, Vol. I: 195 & Vol. II); Pierre-Yves Manguin (1980), *"The Southeast Asian Ship: An Historical Approach,"* *JSEAS* 11(2); Roxanna M. Brown (2007).

### 4. Iron
- **Blueprint Network vs. History:** Blueprint lists *India* and explicitly notes *"China is not designated as a regular iron supplier in this model."* Historically, **iron was scarce in island Southeast Asia** and reached Melaka from **four main sources**: **Karimata (SW Borneo)**, **China** (cast-iron pans, nails, tools), **Siam**, and **India** (Goa/Konkan/Coromandel) `[Documented fact]` `[Scholarly inference]` (Pires 1944; Reid 2011; Loureiro 2010).
- **Origins:** `[Documented fact]` **Karimata** (off SW Borneo) was the premier archipelago exporter of forged *parangs*, axes, and iron blades to Java and the Malay world (Reid 2011). **China** exported cast-iron cooking pans (*kuali*), nails, and ironware—noted in 1514–1515 Portuguese reports from Jorge Álvares' China voyage and Ming trade lists (Pires 1944; Ma Huan 1970, Mills notes). **India** (e.g., Goa/Deccan and Coromandel) exported iron and steel westward to Aden and eastward into the Bay of Bengal (Pires 1944, Vol. I).
- **Merchant Communities & Routes:** `[Documented fact]` **Bornean/Javanese** traders (Syahbandar #3) carried archipelago ironware; **Chinese** and **Siamese** junks (Syahbandar #4) carried Chinese cast-iron pans/nails; **Indian** (Keling/Gujarati/Deccani) ships (Syahbandars #1–2) carried Indian iron and steel.
- **Monsoon Season:** `[Scholarly inference]` **Indian** iron arrived on the **SW monsoon** (May–Sep); **Chinese/Siamese** ironware on the **NE monsoon** (Nov–Mar); **Bornean/Karimata** iron on regional winds (roughly Aug–Oct / E–SE).
- **Measurement & Pricing:** `[Documented fact]` Bulk iron was weighed on Melaka's **large *bahar*** (~210.3 kg) (Nunes 1554, fol. 31); finished utensils (Chinese pans, Karimata *parangs*) were traded by count/bundle `[Scholarly inference]`. No exact 1511 Melaka price per *bahar* of iron is preserved in Pires.
- **Gameplay Notes:** `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` Keeping **India** as the primary gameplay iron route (per blueprint) is a clean design simplification, while occasionally introducing a **Karimata/Borneo** or **unauthorized Chinese ironware** shipment creates great mid-game inspection surprises (e.g., undeclared iron nails or weapon blades hidden inside mixed cargo as 1511 military tensions rise).
- **Sources:** António Nunes, *Livro dos Pesos da India* (1554, fol. 31); Anthony Reid (2011), *Chinese Circulations* (Duke UP); Tomé Pires, *Suma Oriental* (1944); Ma Huan, *Yingya Shenglan* (Mills ed., 1970); Portuguese Malacca factory reports (1515) on Jorge Álvares' China voyage.

### 5. Copper
- **Blueprint Network vs. History:** Blueprint lists *China*. Historically, copper reached Melaka from **both China** (cast copperware, copper cash coins, raw copper) **and Japan via Ryukyuan (*Lequjos*) intermediaries**, with secondary cast copper from **Siam** and West Asia/Europe via Aden `[Documented fact]` (Pires 1944; Kondo 1985).
- **Origins:** `[Documented fact]` **China** produced copper utensils, gongs, and massive quantities of copper cash (*cash*/*pitis* used as small change across Java and Sumatra) (Ma Huan 1970; Pires 1944). **Japan** was a major primary copper producer, carried exclusively to Melaka via the **Ryukyu Kingdom**: Pires writes that *"the Lequjos go to Japan in seven or eight days ... and trade it for gold and copper. All that comes from the Lequeos is brought by them from Japan"* (Pires 1944, Vol. I; Kondo 1985). **Siam** also sent *"vessels of cast copper"* to Melaka (Pires 1944, Vol. I: 108), and Middle Eastern copper flowed via Cairo/Aden to India (Pires 1944, Vol. I: 13).
- **Merchant Communities & Routes:** `[Documented fact]` **Chinese** and **Ryukyuan (*Lequjos*)** merchants under **Syahbandar #4** brought East Asian copper to Melaka, where **Bengali, Keling (Coromandel), and Gujarati** merchants bought it for re-export to India (Pires 1944; Mukherjee 2020).
- **Monsoon Season:** `[Documented fact]` Arrived on the **NE monsoon** (roughly **November–March/April**) aboard Chinese, Ryukyuan, and Siamese junks (Pires 1944; Meilink-Roelofsz 1962).
- **Measurement & Pricing:** `[Scholarly inference]` Bulk copper ingots/bars were weighed by the *bahar* / *kati* on the municipal *dacing*, while Chinese copper cash circulated in strung bundles (*strings* of 1,000 coins in Java). Exact 1511 Melaka unit prices per *bahar* of copper are not tabulated in Pires.
- **Gameplay Notes:** `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` Copper is a **reverse-flow metal**: arriving from the East (China/Ryukyu) on the NE monsoon and bought up by Indian merchants departing west. Grouping Ryukyuan-carried Japanese copper under the "China/East Asia" network fits the blueprint well, while a rare **Ryukyuan ship** carrying Japanese copper + gold makes a memorable special-event vessel. Inspection hooks: copper alloyed with cheaper lead/tin, or undeclared copper cash strings.
- **Sources:** Tomé Pires, *Suma Oriental* (1944, Vol. I: 13, 108, 128–131); Osamu Kondo (1985), *"Japan and the Indian Ocean at the Time of the Mughal Empire"*; Ma Huan, *Yingya Shenglan* (1970); Rila Mukherjee (2020).

### 6. Nutmeg (and Mace)
- **Blueprint Network vs. History:** Blueprint lists *Java*. Historically, **nutmeg (*Myristica fragrans*) and its aril, mace, grew exclusively in the tiny Banda Islands** (Maluku)—NOT in Java—but **Javanese merchants** (especially from Gresik and Tuban) alongside Melaka Malays were the primary carriers who brought Banda nutmeg to Melaka `[Documented fact]` (Pires 1944, Vol. I: 204–206; Villiers 1990).
- **Origins:** `[Documented fact]` Pires records the Melaka merchants' proverb: *"God made Timor for sandalwood, and Banda for mace [and nutmeg], and the Moluccas for cloves, and this merchandise is not known anywhere else in the world except in these places"* (Pires 1944, Vol. I: 204).
- **Merchant Communities & Routes:** `[Documented fact]` **Javanese** junks from Gresik/Tuban and **Malay** junks from Melaka (Syahbandar #3) sailed east to Banda carrying Indian textiles (primarily Gujarati cloth, then Bengali, then Keling) and Javanese rice, exchanging them for nutmeg and mace, then returning to Melaka. From Melaka, **Gujarati, Arab, Persian, Bengali, and Keling** merchants shipped nutmeg/mace westward to India, Aden, Cairo, and Venice, while **Chinese** junks took smaller amounts east (Pires 1944; Barbosa 1918; Rasulid *Mulakhkhas al-Fitan*, Rex Smith 2006).
- **Monsoon Season:** `[Documented fact]` Junks left Melaka/Java for Banda on the West/NW monsoon (Nov–Mar) and **returned to Melaka on the East/SE monsoon (roughly May–September)** (Pires 1944; Villiers 1990).
- **Measurement & Pricing:** `[Documented fact]` Weighed on Melaka's **large *bahar*** (~210.3 kg; Pires notes the Banda *bahar* was slightly heavier, ~550 lbs vs. Melaka's ~530–540 *arráteis*) (Pires 1944, Vol. I: 206; Nunes 1554). Pires reports that in Banda **1 *bahar* of mace cost 3–3½ (some 4) *cruzados*** in goods, **1 *bahar* of mace equaled 1 *bahar* of cloves**, and **1 *bahar* of mace equaled 7 *bahars* of nutmeg**, with nutmeg sold only bundled with mace (Pires 1944, Vol. I: 207–208). Pires' direct Melaka price quotation—**9–10 *cruzados* per *bahar* when plentiful and 12 *cruzados* when scarce**—is for **cloves**, not mace directly (Pires 1944, Vol. I: 214). `[Scholarly inference]` Applying the 1:1 mace-to-cloves equivalence implies mace fetched roughly **9–12 *cruzados* per *bahar*** in Melaka, and dividing by 7 implies nutmeg traded around **~1.3–1.7 *cruzados* per *bahar*** (an inference twice removed).
- **Gameplay Notes:** `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` The blueprint's "Java" label is a sensible abstraction for **who sails the ship**, though manifests and dialogue should accurately cite **Banda** as the origin! Inspection hooks: **mace concealed inside nutmeg sacks** (7× value discrepancy—classic high-value tax evasion!), weevil-eaten or un-limed nuts, or weight mismatch between the Banda *bahar* and Melaka's *large bahar*.
- **Sources:** Tomé Pires, *Suma Oriental* (1944, Vol. I: 204–208, 214); Duarte Barbosa (1918); António Nunes (1554, fol. 31); John Villiers (1990), *"Trade and Society in the Banda Islands in the Sixteenth Century,"* *Modern Asian Studies*; G. Rex Smith (2006) on the Rasulid *Mulakhkhas al-Fitan*.

### 7. Pepper
- **Blueprint Network vs. History:** Blueprint lists *India*. This is the **largest historical divergence** in the blueprint: while **Malabar (Calicut/Cochin)** was the world's most famous pepper exporter to the West, **Melaka's own pepper supply came predominantly from Sumatra** (Pedir, Pasai, Kampar, Indragiri), **Kedah**, and **Sunda (West Java/Banten)**, with Malabar pepper mostly bypassing Melaka or moving on Indian Ocean routes `[Documented fact]` (Pires 1944; Fitriansyach & Wijaya 2024).
- **Origins:** `[Documented fact]` **Northern and eastern Sumatra** (Pedir, Pasai, Kampar, Indragiri) supplied huge volumes to Melaka; **Sunda (Banten/Pontang/Sunda Kalapa)** produced ~**1,000 *bahars*/year** of high-grade pepper rivaling Cochin and shipped mainly to Melaka (Pires 1944; Fitriansyach & Wijaya 2024); **Kedah** produced ~**400 *bahars*/year** (Pires 1944, Vol. I). **Malabar (Calicut/Cochin)** was the giant of Indian pepper, trading directly with Aden, Cambay, and occasionally eastern routes (Pires 1944; Barbosa 1918).
- **Merchant Communities & Routes:** `[Documented fact]` **Sumatran (Pasai/Pedir/Kampar)**, **Sundanese/Javanese**, and **Malay** craft brought archipelago pepper to Melaka (Syahbandars #2 and #3). **China was Melaka's #1 pepper buyer**: Pires writes that Chinese merchants *"will buy ten junk-loads a year if as many go there ... Their whole idea is pepper"* (Pires 1944, Vol. I: 122–125). **Gujarati, Bengali, and Keling** ships also bought Sumatran/Melaka pepper for India and the Red Sea.
- **Monsoon Season:** `[Scholarly inference]` Sumatran and Kedah pepper arrived **across much of the year** via Strait craft; Sunda pepper arrived primarily on the E/SE monsoon (May–Sep); Malabar ships (when calling) arrived on the **SW monsoon** (May–Sep). Outbound pepper junks departed for China on the **SW monsoon** (roughly May–June).
- **Measurement & Pricing:** `[Documented fact]` Weighed on Melaka's **large *bahar*** (~210.3 kg) (Nunes 1554, fol. 31). Pires does not record a unit price per *bahar* for pepper in Sunda or Melaka (the "8 *mates* = 300 *calains* = 9 *cruzados*" figure in Pires 1944, Vol. I: 169 is the exchange rate of a Sunda gold *tumdaya*, not a pepper price); in China (Canton/Tamão), pepper was taxed at **20%**—double the 10% rate on general merchandise—and bartered against silk on voyages yielding up to 3-for-1 returns (Pires 1944, Vol. I: 123, Vol. II: 284).
- **Gameplay Notes:** `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` Consider broadening Pepper's gameplay supply to include **regional Sumatran/Sunda vessels** alongside Indian ships—it creates a vivid strategic loop where Melaka buys Sumatran/Sunda pepper cheaply and sells it to **Chinese junks** arriving on the NE monsoon! Inspection hooks: damp pepper (water-soaked to inflate weight on the *dacing*), unripe/shrivelled berries or black gravel mixed in, or Sunda grade passed off as cheaper Sumatran (or vice versa).
- **Sources:** Tomé Pires, *Suma Oriental* (1944, Vol. I: 108, 122–125, 135–170); Duarte Barbosa (1918); António Nunes (1554, fol. 31); Ashanty Thania Fitriansyach & Daya Negri Wijaya (2024), *"Perdagangan Lada di Sunda Abad XVI,"* *NUANSA* 21(2).

### 8. Sandalwood
- **Blueprint Network vs. History:** Blueprint lists *Java*. Historically, **white sandalwood (*Santalum album*) came almost exclusively from Timor (and nearby Sumba)**, transshipped via **Malay and Javanese** junks—so "Java" reflects the intermediary network rather than the botanical origin `[Documented fact]` (Pires 1944, Vol. I: 204; Barbosa 1918).
- **Origins:** `[Documented fact]` **Timor** (and Solor/Sumba) was the sole major source of prized **white sandalwood (*Santalum album*)**, celebrated in Pires' quote (*"God made Timor for sandalwood"*) and Duarte Barbosa's account of Timor (Pires 1944, Vol. I: 204; Barbosa 1918). A distinct species, **red sandalwood (*Pterocarpus santalinus*)**, grew in **South India** and was used primarily as a dye/wood rather than fine incense (Barbosa 1918).
- **Merchant Communities & Routes:** `[Documented fact]` **Malay** and **Javanese** merchants (Syahbandar #3) sailed annually from Melaka and northern Java to Timor, trading iron axes, Indian cloth, and beads for white sandalwood. At Melaka, sandalwood was eagerly bought by **Chinese** junks (for temple incense and medicine), **Gujarati**, **Keling (Coromandel)**, **Bengali**, and **Arab/Aden** merchants (Pires 1944; Mukherjee 2020; Rex Smith 2006).
- **Monsoon Season:** `[Scholarly inference]` Followed the **eastern archipelago monsoon**: outbound from Melaka/Java on the West/NW winds (Nov–Mar), returning to Melaka on the **East/SE winds (roughly May–September)** (Pires 1944).
- **Measurement & Pricing:** `[Documented fact]` Weighed on Melaka's **large *bahar*** (~210.3 kg) (Nunes 1554, fol. 31). `[Scholarly inference]` Graded heavily by oil content and heartwood density (core heartwood vs. pale sapwood); specific 1511 Melaka *cruzado* prices per *bahar* are not explicitly listed in the surviving *Suma Oriental* tables.
- **Gameplay Notes:** `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` High-value aromatic cargo ideal for **Detailed Inspection**: unscrupulous traders could bundle pale, low-fragrance sapwood or cheaper red Indian wood (*Pterocarpus*) inside white Timor heartwood logs, or misdeclare Timor sandalwood as ordinary timber to evade duties.
- **Sources:** Tomé Pires, *Suma Oriental* (1944, Vol. I: 16, 125, 204); Duarte Barbosa, *The Book of Duarte Barbosa* (Dames ed., 1918); António Nunes (1554, fol. 31); G. Rex Smith (2006); Rila Mukherjee (2020).

### 9. Frankincense
- **Blueprint Network vs. History:** Blueprint lists *Middle East*. This is **historically accurate**—frankincense (*Boswellia sacra* / *B. carterii*) was a classic western Indian Ocean luxury resin shipped through **Aden** and **Cambay (Gujarat)** to Melaka and onward to **China** `[Documented fact]` (Pires 1944, Vol. I: 13–16, 125; Wheatley 1959).
- **Origins:** `[Documented fact]` Harvested in **southern Arabia** (Dhofar in Oman, Hadramawt/Shihr in Yemen) and **northern Somaliland / Horn of Africa**, consolidated at **Aden** and **Hormuz** (Pires 1944, Vol. I; Peacock 2024).
- **Merchant Communities & Routes:** `[Documented fact]` Arab, Persian, and Red Sea merchants brought frankincense from Aden/Shihr to **Cambay (Gujarat)** and **Malabar**, where **Gujarati merchant syndicates** (Syahbandar #1)—often partnering with Arab, Persian, and Turkish (*Rumes*) traders—carried it to Melaka (Pires 1944, Vol. I: 13–16, 42–45). At Melaka, **Chinese** junks were major buyers: Pires notes the Chinese *"will buy a great deal of incense"* (Pires 1944, Vol. I: 125; Wheatley 1959).
- **Monsoon Season:** `[Documented fact]` Arrived at Melaka on the **SW monsoon (roughly May–September)** aboard Gujarati and western Indian Ocean ships departing Cambay in April (Tibbetts 1971; Pires 1944).
- **Measurement & Pricing:** `[Documented fact]` Weighed on Melaka's **large *bahar*** (~210.3 kg)—António Nunes explicitly lists *"incense"* (*incenso*) and *"myrrh"* under the *baar grande* (Nunes 1554, fol. 31). `[Scholarly inference]` Graded by color and purity (translucent pale "tears" commanding top prices over dark/dusty resin); exact 1511 Melaka *cruzado* prices per *bahar* are not preserved in Pires.
- **Gameplay Notes:** `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` Frankincense creates a neat **west-to-east relay**: arrives from the Middle East/Gujarat on the SW monsoon, stored in Melaka's *gudang*, and sold to **Chinese junks** during the NE monsoon. Inspection hooks: **Southeast Asian pine resin or cheap Sumatran dammar/benzoin substituted for Arabian frankincense**—a documented historical practice in China-bound trade (Wheatley 1959; Heng 2009)!
- **Sources:** Tomé Pires, *Suma Oriental* (1944, Vol. I: 13–16, 42–45, 125); António Nunes (1554, fol. 31); Paul Wheatley (1959), *"Geographical Notes on Some Commodities Involved in Sung Maritime Trade"*; Derek Heng (2009); A. C. S. Peacock (2024).

### 10. Silk
- **Blueprint Network vs. History:** Blueprint lists *China*. This is **historically accurate**—China was the overwhelmingly dominant supplier of both **raw white silk** and **woven silk textiles** (damask, brocade, satin) at Melaka `[Documented fact]` (Pires 1944; Barbosa 1918).
- **Origins:** `[Documented fact]` **China** (primarily Jiangnan/Zhejiang/Fujian/Guangdong workshops) exported both raw white silk thread (*seda branca*) and finished luxury fabrics—damasks, satins, brocades, and taffetas—through southern ports near **Canton (Guangzhou / Tamão)** and **Zhangzhou (Fujian)** (Pires 1944, Vol. I; Loureiro 2010). Minor quantities of silk also arrived from **Bengal** or via **Ryukyu** transit `[Scholarly inference]`.
- **Merchant Communities & Routes:** `[Documented fact]` **Chinese** junks (Guangdong and Fujian/*Chancheo*) and **Ryukyuan (*Lequjos*)** vessels under **Syahbandar #4** brought silk to Melaka. There it was snapped up by **Gujarati** ships (*"enormous quantities of white silk"*, Pires 1944), **Keling (Coromandel)**, **Bengali**, and **Arab/Aden** merchants for re-export to India and the Middle East (Pires 1944; Mukherjee 2020; Rex Smith 2006).
- **Monsoon Season:** `[Documented fact]` Arrived on the **NE monsoon (roughly November–March/April)** from China and Ryukyu; re-exported westward on the same NE monsoon or held in Melaka's *gudang* for the next western fleet (Pires 1944; Meilink-Roelofsz 1962).
- **Measurement & Pricing:** `[Documented fact]` **Raw Chinese silk** was weighed on Melaka's **small *bahar*** (~183.6 kg)—Nunes explicitly lists *"China silk"* and *"anafáia"* (first-spun silk yarn) under the *baar pequeno* (Nunes 1554, fol. 31; Pinto 2012). **Woven silk textiles** (damasks, brocades) were counted by piece/bolt `[Scholarly inference]`. Exact 1511 Melaka *cruzado* prices per *bahar* of raw silk are not preserved in Pires, though Portuguese reports (1515) on Jorge Álvares' China voyage highlight white silk, damask, and brocade as the most lucrative return cargoes.
- **Gameplay Notes:** `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` Silk is a **high-value, moisture-sensitive luxury** where **Small *Bahar* vs. Large *Bahar*** weighing errors are costly. Inspection hooks: seawater/damp staining hidden inside outer dry wrappings, raw silk padded with heavy damp cores, or lower-grade waste silk (*anafáia*) declared as fine white raw silk.
- **Sources:** Tomé Pires, *Suma Oriental* (1944, Vols. I–II); António Nunes (1554, fol. 31); Paulo Jorge de Sousa Pinto (2012); Rui Manuel Loureiro (2010); Rila Mukherjee (2020); G. Rex Smith (2006).

### 11. Cloth (Cotton Textiles)
- **Blueprint Network vs. History:** Blueprint lists *India*. This is **historically accurate and economically foundational**—Indian cotton textiles were the **universal currency of the Indonesian spice trade** `[Documented fact]` (Pires 1944; Meilink-Roelofsz 1962; Reid 1988).
- **Origins:** `[Documented fact]` Three distinct Indian regions supplied Melaka's cloth market, each with specialized grades (Pires 1944, Vol. I; Barbosa 1918):
  1. **Gujarat (Cambay):** Over 30 varieties of dyed/printed cottons and silk-cotton *patola*—the single most prized cloth in Melaka and the Spice Islands (Banda/Moluccas). Pires famously wrote: *"Malacca cannot live without Cambay, nor Cambay without Malacca"* (Pires 1944, Vol. I: 45).
  2. **Coromandel Coast (Pulicat):** Coarse and patterned **Keling cloth** (*pintados*/chintz), vital for everyday regional trade and spice barter.
  3. **Bengal:** Fine white cotton muslins (*sinabafas* / *beathila*) and bleached cloths.
  *(Secondary regional supply: **Siam** also sent cheap coarse cotton cloth to Melaka [Pires 1944, Vol. I: 108].)*
- **Merchant Communities & Routes:** `[Documented fact]` **Gujarati** merchants (Syahbandar #1) sent **4 major ships/year** (worth **15,000–30,000 *cruzados*** each) plus one giant Cambay flagship worth **70,000–80,000 *cruzados***; **Keling (Coromandel)** merchants (Syahbandar #2) sent 3–4 ships/year (12,000–15,000 *cruzados* each) plus 1–2 great Pulicat ships worth **80,000–90,000 *cruzados***; **Bengali** merchants (Syahbandar #2) sent 4–6 ships/year (up to 80,000–90,000 *cruzados* for the largest vessels) (Pires 1944, Vol. II; Mukherjee 2020). At Melaka, Javanese and Malay merchants bought Indian cloth to barter for **cloves, nutmeg, mace, pepper, and sandalwood** in the archipelago.
- **Monsoon Season:** `[Documented fact]` Arrived on the **SW monsoon (roughly May–September)**, departing Gujarat around mid-April and Coromandel/Bengal in spring–early summer (Tibbetts 1971; Pires 1944).
- **Measurement & Pricing:** `[Documented fact]` Counted in ***corge*** / ***kodi*** (**20 pieces/cloths per *corge***) and packed in tightly wrapped bales (*fardos*). Subject to Melaka's **6% customs duty** plus customary official gifts of **1 cloth per 100** (which Gujaratis avoided via lump-sum ship valuation of ≥21,000 *cruzados*) (Pires 1944, Vol. II).
- **Gameplay Notes:** `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` Cloth is the **lifeblood of Melaka's tax revenue and spice purchasing power**—losing a Pulicat or Cambay cloth ship is catastrophic for both customs receipts and the eastern spice fleet! Inspection hooks: **mislabeled regional origin** (cheap coarse Siamese or Keling cloth packed inside bales declared as fine Gujarati *patola* or Bengali muslin—or the reverse to evade the 6% ad valorem tax!), short counts inside a *corge* (18 pieces instead of 20), or water-damaged inner bolts.
- **Sources:** Tomé Pires, *Suma Oriental* (1944, Vol. I: 42–45, 108 & Vol. II); Duarte Barbosa (1918); M. A. P. Meilink-Roelofsz (1962); Rila Mukherjee (2020); Anthony Reid (1988).

### 12. Porcelain (and Trade Ceramics)
- **Blueprint Network vs. History:** Blueprint lists *China* and notes *"large porcelain jars and small porcelain bowls belong to the same commodity category. A large jar may contain bowls or other goods."* This aligns closely with both **written sources and shipwreck archaeology** from c. 1490–1515 `[Documented fact]` (Pires 1944; Brown 2007; Orillaneda 2016; Welch 2011).
- **Origins:** `[Documented fact]` After the 15th-century "Ming Gap," Chinese blue-and-white porcelain exports surged dramatically during the **Hongzhi reign (1488–1505)** and continued at peak volume through **1511**, as proven by the **Brunei, Lena Shoal, and Santa Cruz shipwrecks** (each carrying thousands of intact pieces, ~90% Chinese blue-and-white from **Jingdezhen**, plus **Longquan celadon** and **Guangdong** brown-glazed storage jars) (Brown 2007; Orillaneda 2016; Welch 2011). Secondary Southeast Asian ceramics—**Thai** (Sawankhalok/Si Satchanalai, Sukhothai, Maenam Noi jars), **Vietnamese** (Chu Dau blue-and-white), and **Burmese** (*Martaban* stoneware jars & celadon plates)—traveled alongside Chinese wares (Orillaneda 2016; Museum of Asian Art UM 2023).
- **Merchant Communities & Routes:** `[Documented fact]` **Chinese** and **Ryukyuan** junks (Syahbandar #4) and Southeast Asian lashed-lug junks (Malay, Siamese, Bornean/Filipino) carried ceramics to Melaka. From Melaka, porcelain was re-exported across the Malay Archipelago, **India** (Gujarat, Bengal, Coromandel), **Aden**, and **Egypt** (Pires 1944; Mukherjee 2020; Welch 2011).
- **Monsoon Season:** `[Documented fact]` Arrived from China and Siam on the **NE monsoon (roughly November–March/April)** (Pires 1944; Meilink-Roelofsz 1962).
- **Measurement & Pricing:** `[Scholarly inference]` Traded by **piece, set, or nested bundle/jar** rather than by weight, priced by kiln quality, glaze, size, and intactness. Exact 1511 Melaka unit prices are not listed in Pires, though Portuguese China-voyage reports (1515) rank porcelain alongside white silk as a premier high-margin Chinese export.
- **Gameplay Notes:** `[Documented fact]` `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` **Nested jar packing is historically attested**: large stoneware storage jars (*martaban* / Guangdong jars) served simultaneously as **trade goods and shipping containers** holding water, pickled foods, ginger, oil, or **tightly nested stacks of smaller porcelain bowls/dishes** (Museum of Asian Art UM 2023; Orillaneda 2016). This directly validates the blueprint's tactile inspection mechanic! Additional historical hook: Roxanna Brown (2007) argues that the **1511 fall of Melaka and 1512 destruction of the Javanese/Malay junk fleet** triggered an abrupt shift to smaller cargoes and lower-quality Zhengde-period wares—a rich late-game narrative detail. Inspection hooks: undeclared high-grade blue-and-white bowls, silk, or contraband spices concealed inside jars declared as coarse stoneware or pickled provisions; kiln flaws/cracks hidden mid-stack.
- **Sources:** Roxanna M. Brown (2007), *"Shipwreck Ceramics and the Fall of Melaka"*; Bobby C. Orillaneda (2016), *"Insights into Southeast Asian Maritime Trade during the 15th Century: Asian Ceramics from the Santa Cruz Shipwreck"*; Patricia Bjaaland Welch (2011), *"The Brunei Shipwreck"*; Tomé Pires, *Suma Oriental* (1944); Museum of Asian Art, University of Malaya (2023).

### 13. Glassware (Vessels, Mirrors, and Glass Beads)
- **Blueprint Network vs. History:** Blueprint lists *Middle East*. Historically, **blown glass vessels, rosewater flasks, and mirrors** indeed flowed from the **Mediterranean and Middle East (Venice, Cairo/Egypt, Syria/Levant, Persia)** via Aden and Cambay, while **glass beads and bangles** also came from **Gujarat/Cambay**, **South India**, and **China** `[Documented fact]` (Pires 1944, Vol. I: 13–16; Francis 2002; Welch 2011).
- **Origins:** `[Documented fact]` Pires explicitly lists *"glass of all kinds and colours ... all sorts of glass beads ... rosewater ... many mirrors"* traveling from **Venice/Alexandria/Cairo** via **Jeddah and Aden** to **Cambay, Malabar, Bengal, Pegu, Siam**, and onward to Melaka (Pires 1944, Vol. I: 13). **Cambay (Gujarat)** was also a famous bead-making hub (carnelian and glass beads), and **China** produced glass beads and bracelets found in c. 1500 Southeast Asian shipwrecks such as the **Brunei** and **Lena Shoal** wrecks (Francis 2002; Welch 2011).
- **Merchant Communities & Routes:** `[Documented fact]` **Arab, Persian, Egyptian, and Gujarati** merchants (Syahbandar #1) carried Middle Eastern/Venetian glassware, mirrors, rosewater flasks, and Cambay beads to Melaka, where **Malay, Javanese, Bornean, and Chinese** traders bought them (Pires notes Chinese junks bought *"red beads"* and *"carnelians from Cambay"* at Melaka) (Pires 1944, Vol. I: 13, 125).
- **Monsoon Season:** `[Documented fact]` Western/Middle Eastern and Gujarati glassware arrived on the **SW monsoon (roughly May–September)**; Chinese glass beads/bangles arrived on the **NE monsoon (Nov–Mar)** (Pires 1944; Tibbetts 1971).
- **Measurement & Pricing:** `[Scholarly inference]` Fragile blown glassware and mirrors were traded by **piece or crate**, while glass beads were traded by **string, bundle, or weight** (rosewater itself—often shipped in glass flasks—was weighed on Melaka's **small *bahar*** [~183.6 kg] per Nunes 1554, fol. 31). Exact 1511 Melaka unit prices for glassware are not preserved in Pires.
- **Gameplay Notes:** `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` Glassware is a **fragile, high-margin western luxury** that pairs naturally with Frankincense on Middle Eastern/Gujarati ships. Inspection hooks: **breakage/damage** during unloading or rough anchorage waiting (creating urgency for careful berthing), **cheap glass beads passed off as Cambay carnelian or coral**, or hollow/false-bottomed glassware crates hiding high-value western drugs (opium, saffron, rosewater).
- **Sources:** Tomé Pires, *Suma Oriental* (1944, Vol. I: 13–16, 125); António Nunes (1554, fol. 31); Peter Francis Jr. (2002), *Asia's Maritime Bead Trade: 300 B.C. to the Present* (University of Hawai'i Press); Patricia Bjaaland Welch (2011); Ma Huan, *Yingya Shenglan* (1970).

## 4. Key Gameplay & Design Takeaways for *Port Master: Melaka 1511*

1. **`[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` The Two-Stroke Entrepôt Loop:** Because Melaka produced almost nothing except **Tin**, its core economic loop is **inter-monsoon exchange**:
   - **SW Monsoon (West → Melaka):** **Cloth, Frankincense, Glassware, Indian Iron** (plus **Rice/Timber** from Pegu & Bengal) arrive and pay the **6% ad valorem customs duty**.
   - **E/SE Monsoon (Archipelago → Melaka):** **Rice, Timber, Nutmeg/Mace, Sandalwood, Sunda/Sumatra Pepper** arrive from Java, Banda, Timor, and Sumatra.
   - **NE Monsoon (East → Melaka):** **Silk, Porcelain, Copper** (plus **Rice/Coarse Cloth** from Siam) arrive from China, Ryukyu, and Siam, eager to buy **Pepper, Tin, Frankincense, and Sandalwood**.
2. **`[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` High-Signal Detailed Inspection Hooks (Grounded in Sources):**
   - **Dual *Bahar* Weight Trap:** Large *bahar* (~210.3 kg: spices, sandalwood, frankincense, iron) vs. Small *bahar* (~183.6 kg: tin, raw silk, rosewater). A captain weighing on the wrong *dacing* standard creates a ~14.5% discrepancy.
   - **Nested Porcelain Jars:** Historically attested storage jars packing nested bowls—or hiding undeclared luxury cargo (silk, spices).
   - **Grade & Substitution Fraud:** Mace hidden in nutmeg (7× value); Southeast Asian dammar/pine resin sold as Arabian frankincense; sapwood/red wood mixed into white Timor sandalwood; coarse cloth packed in Gujarati/Bengali bales; water-soaked pepper.
   - **Food-Duty Smuggling:** Duty-light rice cargoes concealing taxable luxury goods underneath.
3. **`[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` Blueprint Refinements Worth Considering:**
   - Add **Sumatra/Sunda** as a regional source of **Pepper** alongside India so Melaka's historic role as China's pepper depot feels authentic.
   - Keep **Java** as the *carrier* for **Nutmeg** and **Sandalwood**, while naming **Banda** and **Timor** on manifests/manual entries so players can spot forged provenance!
   - Keep **China** out of regular **Iron** supply (per blueprint v0.5), but allow rare **Karimata/Borneo** or contraband Chinese cast-iron shipments as mid/late-game inspection cases.
