# The Port Official of Melaka c. 1511: Title, Powers, Duties and Customs Procedure

Research note for blueprint §02 ("The precise historical title and extent of this authority require research") and open question 6 in §15 (keeping the player's powers plausible and naming the abstractions).

**Labels used on every point**

- **[FACT]**: a primary source says it, quoted or closely paraphrased, with a page reference.
- **[INFERENCE]**: a reasonable reading of the sources, by me or by a named modern scholar. It is not stated outright in a primary text.
- **[GAMEPLAY]**: a suggested design abstraction. It is not a historical claim.

**What I checked directly.** The Pires, Albuquerque, Sejarah Melayu and Undang-Undang Melaka points were checked against the editions linked or listed in §8. The separate Maritime Laws edition by Winstedt and de Josselin de Jong was not checked directly.

---

## 1. Summary

1. **The title is *shahbandar*** (Pires writes *xabamdar*; Persian *shāh-bandar*, "lord of the port"). **[FACT]** Pires, *Suma Oriental* (Cortesão ed.), pp. 264–5; Yule & Burnell, *Hobson-Jobson*, s.v. "Shabunder".
2. **Melaka had four shahbandars, one for each group of merchant nations**, and the Gujarati shahbandar was "the most important of all". **[FACT]** Pires, p. 265; *Commentaries of Afonso Dalboquerque*, vol. III, pp. 87–8.
3. **The shahbandar was a broker and host, not the sovereign of the harbour.** He received ship captains, presented them to the bendahara, allotted warehouses, dispatched their goods, arranged lodging and elephants, and set the level of "presents". **[FACT]** Pires, pp. 265, 273.
4. **Other offices held the hard powers.** The **temenggung** received the dues and sat as Customs Judge. The **laksamana** had jurisdiction over everyone at sea. The **bendahara** was chief justice and controlled the king's revenue. **[FACT]** Pires, pp. 264–5, 273; *Commentaries* III, p. 88.
5. **Dues:** western merchandise generally paid 6%; eastern merchandise paid assessed presents instead. Presents could also accompany dues; Pires reports 1–2% in one passage, sometimes more, not a universal rate. He records a separate weighing fee and distinguishes the valuation panel used for some large ships from the later collective bidding for cargo. **[FACT]** Pires, pp. 270–4.
6. **Player role (decided):** the player is a shahbandar. Port-wide berthing authority is marked as a gameplay abstraction, and dues and rulings are issued in the temenggung's name (see §6). **[GAMEPLAY]**

| Cortesão calls Pires' passage "the first European description of the Malacca high officials, Bendahara, Laksamana, Temenggong and Xabandar". | FACT (editor's note) | Cortesão, note 1 to p. 264 |

| "Harbour master" / "port master" is a fair English gloss, but it overstates his control of the water (see §4). | INFERENCE | From Pires, p. 265 (laksamana's jurisdiction at sea) |

## 3. Number, communities served, appointment

**Four shahbandars, one per group of nations [FACT, Pires p. 265]:**

| # | Communities (Pires' spelling → modern) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *Guzarates*: Gujaratis (Cambay) | "the most important of all" (Pires, p. 265). The *Commentaries* call him "the mainstay of all the merchants of Cambaya, a man of great credit with the king" (III, p. 70). |
| 2 | *Bunuaqujlim* (Kling/Coromandel and Tamil coast), Bengalis, Pegu, Pasai | Pires says the Klings had "more of the Malacca trade than any other nation" (p. 272). |
| 3 | Javanese, Moluccans, Banda, Palembang, *Tamjompura* (Tanjungpura), *Luções* (Luzon) | Eastern archipelago |
| 4 | Chinese, *Lequeos* (Ryukyu), *Chancheo* (Zhangzhou/Fujian), Champa | Far East |

- The *Commentaries* agree on four, "one of China, another of Java, another of Cambaya, another of Bengala", and say "all the lands were divided among these four men". **[FACT]** *Commentaries* III, pp. 87–8.
- "Each man applies to [the Xabamdar] of his nation when he comes to Malacca with merchandise or messages." **[FACT]** Pires, p. 265.
- **Shahbandars belonged to the communities they served:** the xabandares "are sympathetic to the merchants and of the same nations as the merchants". **[FACT]** Pires, p. 273. The Sejarah Melayu's port master Raja Mendaliar is "a certain man of Kalinga" (a Kling), "easily the richest man of his time in Malaka". **[FACT]** Brown, p. 160.
- **Appointment:** The *Undang-Undang Melaka* explicitly directs the ruler to appoint the syahbandar, alongside the bendahara, temenggung and penghulu bendahari. This is the legal rule, not a record of who nominated or appointed any one of the four. Pires calls the positions “municipal offices” (p. 265); the *Sejarah Melayu* says Raja Mendaliar “had been made Master of the Port” without naming the appointing person (Brown, p. 160). The Annals place the shahbandar and revenue staff under the *Penghulu Bendahari* (Treasurer) in their account of court offices (Brown, p. 58). **[FACT]** Liaw, §0.1; Pires, p. 265; Brown, pp. 58, 160.
- **Politics:** shahbandars could be politically powerful. The Gujarati shahbandar advised Sultan Mahmud against peace with the Portuguese in 1511, and "the king was well pleased with the advice" (*Commentaries* III, p. 70). Raja Mendaliar's bribe to Laksamana Khoja Husain and his false charge led to the execution of Bendahara Sri Maharaja (c. 1510). Mendaliar was then executed for false accusation (Brown, pp. 160–4). **[FACT]**
- After the conquest, Albuquerque appointed community "governors of the land": Ninachatu for the Hindus, a *caciz* for the Moors, Regunecerage and Tuão Calascar for Javanese districts, with Ruy de Araújo deciding their disputes. These were Portuguese-era community heads, not the sultanate's shahbandars. **[FACT]** *Commentaries* III, pp. 166–7.

## 4. What the shahbandar controlled, and what other offices controlled

### 4.1 The shahbandar's documented functions

Pires, p. 265: "They are the men who receive the captains of the junks, each one according as he is under his jurisdiction. These men present them to the Bemdara, allot them warehouses, dispatch their merchandise, provide them with lodging if they have documents, and give orders for the elephants." **[FACT]**

| Function | Status | Source / reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Receive arriving captains of his nations | FACT | Pires, p. 265 |
| **Audience:** present captains to the bendahara | FACT | Pires, p. 265. The shahbandar was the merchant's way in to the bendahara, not an independent judge. |
| **Warehousing:** allot warehouses (godowns) | FACT | Pires, p. 265 |
| Dispatch (clear and forward) merchandise | FACT | Pires, p. 265 |
| Lodging for those with documents (letters/credentials) | FACT | Pires, p. 265 |
| Overland transport: "give orders for the elephants" | FACT | Pires, p. 265 |
| **Presents:** decide how large the presents are | FACT | "According as the Xabandar decides, so the merchants pay" (Pires, p. 273) |
| Receive a present himself | FACT | Pires, pp. 270, 273 |
| Collect the later due on junks sold in Melaka (15 per 300 cruzados) for the king | FACT | "this the xabandares of the different nations collected for the king" (Pires, p. 274) |
| **Berthing / anchorage allocation** | INFERENCE | Barbara Watson Andaya credits the shahbandar with supervising anchoring (p. 90); Pires describes the shahbandar receiving captains and the laksamana having broad jurisdiction at sea (pp. 264–5), but does not say who chose individual berths or how ships were queued. |
| **Main customs dues (the 6%)** | Not his: FACT | The temenggung "is also the one who receives the dues on the merchandise" (Pires, p. 265). |
| **Weights and measures** | FACT | The land digest assigns rules on *gantang*, *cupak*, *kati*, *tahil*, and market regulation to the shahbandar (Liaw, §29). Pires instead names the king's secretaries and receivers for the weighing due, and a separate royal office for assaying gold (pp. 274, 276). Setting standards is distinct from collecting the fee or doing the assay. |
| Trade disputes and customs enforcement | FACT | The land digest gives the shahbandar jurisdiction over disputes under market and vessel rules (§29). If a captain who evaded customs returned, it prescribes confiscation or twice the duty and reserves pardon to the ruler upon the shahbandar's intercession (§24.1). This does not make the shahbandar the Customs Judge: the *Commentaries* give that role to the temenggung (III, pp. 87–8). |

### 4.2 The other offices a port official deals with

| Office | Powers relevant to the port | Source | Label |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sultan** | Final authority. Consents to cargo valuations ("with the consent of the king"). Licenses house/garden sales. Receives presents and the junk-sale due. | Pires, pp. 273–4 | FACT |
| **Paduka Raja** (occasional) | "A kind of viceroy, who is next to the king", created only sometimes | Pires, p. 264 | FACT |
| **Bendahara** | "A kind of chief-justice in all civil and criminal affairs. He also has charge of the king's revenue." Can order executions after informing the king. Receives presents. Holds audiences for newly arrived captains. | Pires, pp. 264–5; *Commentaries* III, p. 87 ("Controller of the Treasury, and governs the kingdom") | FACT |
| **Penghulu Bendahari** (Treasurer) | Head of the raja's servants and clerks. "Under him too were the Shahbandar and all those in charge of the revenues of the state." | Brown, *Sejarah Melayu*, p. 58 | FACT |
| **Temenggung** | "Chief magistrate in the city". Has the guard and handles prison cases. "Receives the dues on the merchandise". Customs Judge at valuations. | Pires, pp. 265, 273; *Commentaries* III, p. 88 | FACT |
| **Laksamana** | Admiral. "Everybody at sea, and junks and lancharas are under this man's jurisdiction." King's guard. Receives presents. | Pires, pp. 264–5 | FACT |

- **Access to the sultan is not a routine arrival step.** Pires says captains were presented to the bendahara (p. 265). The Gujarati shahbandar's direct advice to the ruler is a specific political episode (*Commentaries* III, p. 70); the Annals put royal summons at court under the Treasurer (Brown, pp. 57–8). **[FACT for the accounts; INFERENCE against treating royal audiences as routine]**
- In 1509–11 the customs-judge temenggung was a relative of Bendahara Sri Maharaja: his **brother** according to Pires (p. 273), his **son** Tun Hasan Temenggong according to the Sejarah Melayu (Brown, p. 163). Cortesão identifies Pires' "Tumungo" with Tun Hasan (note to p. 255). **[FACT: the sources conflict]** Either way, the bendahara's family controlled both the audience and the customs bench. **[INFERENCE]**
- Merchants could be ruined by court intrigue. In 1509 merchants "took him double the present" to win the bendahara over against Diogo Lopes de Sequeira, and also bribed the laksamana and temenggung (Pires, pp. 254–5). **[FACT]**

## 5. Duties and customs procedure (for the reference manual and inspection case)

### 5.1 Rates by origin

| Category | What they paid | Source | Label |
|---|---|---|---|
| **"Of the west"**: Aden, Mecca, Ormuz, Parsees, Gujarat, Chaul, Dabhol, Goa, Calicut, Malabar, Ceylon, Coromandel, Pulicat, Orissa, Bengal, Arakan, Pegu, Siam | **6%** on merchandise | Pires, pp. 272–3 | FACT |
| Presents described after the settled-merchant rates | **Presents** to the king, bendahara, temenggung and the shahbandar of their nation, worth "one or two per cent", at the level the shahbandar decides. "Sometimes they give more", to stay on good terms. Do not treat this as an automatic surcharge for every western or eastern arrival. | Pires, p. 273 | FACT for the passage; INFERENCE for its scope |
| Gujaratis (detail) | 6%. Besides that, "one cloth per hundred" each to bendahara, laksamana, temenggung and shahbandar, which they found "a great oppression". So they chose to have the **whole ship valued** and paid 6% on the valuation. A Gujarat ship was valued at no less than about 21,000 cruzados. | Pires, p. 270 (with Cortesão's note on the figure) | FACT |
| Klings | "Six per cent entry-dues and nothing on coming away" | Pires, p. 272 | FACT |
| Pegu and Siam provisions; all provisions from Tenasserim, Kedah, Pedir, Pasai | Provisions pay a **present**, not dues | Pires, p. 273; "All provisions pay presents and not dues" (p. 274) | FACT |
| Settled merchants (Malays or others who have wives in Melaka) | "They pay three per cent, and besides this a royal due of six per cent in the case of a foreigner, and three in the case of a native." | Pires, p. 273 | FACT (wording is ambiguous; quote it, don't simplify it) |
| **"The entire East"**: Pahang to China, Java, Banda, Moluccas, Palembang, all Sumatra | **No dues. Presents only**, "a reasonable amount, something like dues", estimated by "taxing officials". Presents from China were "larger than from all other parts". | Pires, p. 274 | FACT |
| **Weighing due** | "One calaim was paid on each hundred the merchandise was worth", handled by the king's "secretaries and receivers" on merchandise weighed in or out. Keep the historical unit in the reference manual instead of treating it as 1% in any currency. | Pires, pp. 274–5 | FACT for the reported charge; GAMEPLAY for the manual |
| Sale of a junk in Melaka | 2–3 *tundaias* (taels) of gold per hull to the king. Later decreed at 15 per 300 cruzados (5%), collected by the shahbandars. | Pires, p. 274 | FACT |
| Exports | No general export duty is reported. The Klings pay "nothing on coming away" (p. 272), and the weighing due applies to goods "that came in or went out" (p. 274). | Pires, pp. 272, 274 | FACT. That this applied to everyone is INFERENCE. |

**[INFERENCE]** Do not use the four shahbandar constituencies as tax bands. Pasai is grouped with Bengal for reception, mentioned in the western discussion of provisions, and covered geographically by Pires's broad eastern summary (“all [places] in the island of Sumatra”). The clear manual example is Pasai provisions → present, not a single rate for every Pasai cargo (Pires, pp. 265, 273–4).

### 5.2 Arrival-to-sale clearance (reconstructed sequence)

**[INFERENCE]** The order below is a reconstruction for the manual. Pires gives reception and storage duties and puts unloading and settlement before sale; he does not fix the relative order of the audience, storage, weighing and assessment, or describe a standard manifest or clearance form (pp. 265, 273–4).

| Step | What happens | Label | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ship arrives in the roads. The laksamana has jurisdiction over vessels at sea. | FACT (jurisdiction); who handled the actual anchoring is INFERENCE | Pires, pp. 264–5 |
| 2 | The captain goes to the **shahbandar of his nation**. | FACT | Pires, p. 265 |
| 3 | The shahbandar **presents him to the bendahara** (audience). Envoys with letters get lodging. | FACT | Pires, p. 265 |
| 4 | The shahbandar **allots warehouses** and orders **elephants** for transport. | FACT | Pires, p. 265 |
| 5 | **Unloading** happens immediately: "as soon as the merchants arrive they unload their cargo and pay their dues or presents". | FACT | Pires, p. 273 |
| 6 | **Assessment.** Either (a) normal rates plus presents, with the shahbandar setting the presents, or (b) for big ships, with the king's consent, a **valuation**: "ten merchants, five Klings and five from some other nation, and before the Customs Judge, who was the Tumungam … make the valuation and receive the dues and presents". The point was to stop "each one" taking "his pickings", stop theft by guards, and stop bribery of valuers. | FACT | Pires, pp. 270, 273 |
| 7 | **Weighing.** Pires describes counting a separate weighing due on goods entering or leaving; he does not fix when it was paid in relation to other charges. | FACT | Pires, p. 274 |
| 8 | **Collective sale (the *bazaar* price-fixing).** "Ten or twenty merchants gathered together with the owner of the said merchandise and bid for it, and by the said merchants the price was fixed and divided amongst them all in proportion." Local merchants then resold "at their pleasure". This was done because "time was short and the merchandise considerable". | FACT | Pires, pp. 273–4 |
| 9 | "And this done, they sell their goods freely." | FACT | Pires, p. 273 |

- **Royal first purchase:** none of the sources checked describes a general royal right to buy first. What they document is (a) presents to the king and (b) collective purchase by a merchant panel. **[FACT that it is absent from these texts]** Blueprint §06's "state purchasing" is therefore correctly labelled an abstraction already. A plausible historical hook is the **merchant panel in step 8**, or the ruler's own trading. Pires says the 1509 plotters wanted Sequeira's goods "for the king and Bemdara" (p. 255). **[INFERENCE]**
- **Shipboard jurisdiction is separate.** The land digest gives the nakhoda (ship's master) responsibility for the law at sea, subject to stated limits. It does not make the nakhoda the official who clears arriving cargo for sale. **[FACT for the prescription; INFERENCE for the division of clearance duties]** Liaw, §§9.1–9.2, 29; Pires, pp. 265, 273–4.

### 5.3 Weights and money a manual will need (Pires, pp. 275–7) [FACT]

- Currency: tin *calains* (*calains in timas*). "Every hundred cashes make one calaim". 100 calains = 3 cruzados. Gujarat/Ormuz *xerafins* and Pasai *dramas* also circulated.
- Gold weight: the **tael/tundaia** = 16 *mazes*. The gold **cate** = 20 taels. Gold, silver, musk, edible camphor, calambac and seed-pearls are weighed by the gold cate. A separate, heavier **merchandise cate** existed.
- The **merchandise cate** is 23 taels. Pires gives a **bahar** of 200 merchandise cates for goods including silk and copper, but 205 for pepper, cloves, and other listed goods.
- Gold quality was graded in *mates* (Brunei lowest at 4½–6, Kling and Cochin-China best at 9½+), and the king's single licensed assayer valued it.

**[GAMEPLAY]** For the Writer's inspection case, give a Gujarat ship a pepper tally that uses 200 merchandise cates per bahar. The player can spot the mismatch against the manual (205 for pepper), reweigh the shipment and refer a disputed valuation to the temenggung. Use the 6% on assessed value and show presents separately. The discrepancy, tally format and referral workflow are fictional; the weight standard, rate and customs office are from Pires, pp. 265, 270, 273, 276–7.

## 6. Answer to open question 6: suggested gameplay framing

**Decision (team lead, 2026-11-27):** the player is a **shahbandar**. Port-wide berthing authority is a **gameplay abstraction**, because the sources give the laksamana jurisdiction at sea (Pires, pp. 264–5). Dues and customs rulings are **issued in the temenggung's name**, because he received the dues and sat as Customs Judge (Pires, pp. 265, 273). **[GAMEPLAY]** The table below gives the options that lay behind this decision.

| Mechanic in blueprint | Historical anchor | Label |
|---|---|---|
| **Player title:** *Shahbandar* (e.g. of the Gujaratis, the most important, or a fictional deputy or fifth shahbandar so no real person is displaced) | Pires, p. 265 | GAMEPLAY on a FACT base |
| Receiving captains, warehouse allocation, dispatch, elephants/porters | Pires, p. 265 | GAMEPLAY ≈ FACT |
| **Setting presents** as a dial (merchant goodwill vs. court favour) | Pires, p. 273 | GAMEPLAY ≈ FACT. Good core tension. |
