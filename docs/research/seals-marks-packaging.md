# Seals, marks and cargo packaging in Melaka around 1511

**Bottom line:** Melaka has direct textual evidence for diplomatic seals, port documents of unspecified kind, and merchant accounting. The clearest near-period physical evidence for cargo packaging is regional: porcelain cups found inside stoneware jars at Santa Cruz. Neither line of evidence supplies a standard Melaka cargo seal, a cross-port code for merchant marks, or a surviving sultanate manifest template. [Ma Huan, pp. 108–114][ma]; [Ming Shi-lu, 20 Nov. 1407][ming]; [Pires, II, p. 265][pires]; [Barbosa, II, pp. 175–176][barbosa]; [Orillaneda, pp. 305, 318, 322–325][santa].

**How to read this note:** “Documented fact” identifies what a source actually records or an excavation actually found. Comparisons from other ports or centuries are dated and located; they are not Melaka evidence. “Scholarly inference” identifies interpretations and where applying them to Melaka would be an extrapolation. “Gaps” are choices for the game, not claims that a practice never existed.

## 1. Seals and royal authority

### Documented fact

- **Melaka, early fifteenth century — diplomatic insignia.** Ma Huan reports that Zheng He was ordered to bestow two silver seals on Melaka’s chief, along with imperial edicts and robes of rank. The Ming court record already refers to Melaka and Samudera complaining in 1407 that Siam had taken away the seals and title patents granted to them; the court ordered their return. These are two accounts of diplomatic authority, not necessarily the same episode or pair of seals. Neither describes an impression on cargo. [Ma Huan, pp. 108–109][ma]; [Ming Shi-lu, 20 Nov. 1407, Tai-zong 72.4b–5a][ming].
- **Around 1511 — a reported mark on the goods themselves.** The later *Commentaries*, compiled by Afonso’s son, say that the Gores (Lequeos) trading to Melaka brought little cakes of gold stamped with their king’s seal. The account explicitly says it could not establish whether these were money or stamped to show they had passed through a port. It does not describe a stamp on their containers. [Albuquerque, III, ch. XVIII, pp. 88–89][albu].
- **Later regional images, not Melaka cargo seals.** Gallop reproduces Acehnese Sultan Alauddin Riayat Syah’s impression on a 1602 trading permit (fig. 2) and a round Pahang royal impression on a 1614 treaty (fig. 11). Her wider corpus includes matrices and impressions in lampblack, ink and wax, so neither colorful wax nor any one silhouette should stand for every Malay seal. [Gallop 2004, figs. 2, 11][gallop04]; [Gallop 2019, catalogue description][gallop19].

### Scholarly inference

- Baker and Phongpaichit read the Ming seals and title patents as credentials relevant to trading with China and the Siamese seizure as a possible attempt to disrupt that trade. That interpretation supports a link between diplomatic authentication and access, not a system for sealing jars or recording duty paid. Likewise, the later Aceh permit is evidence of a seal authenticating permission, not of a physical closure. [Baker and Phongpaichit, “Melaka, Ayutthaya and China”][baker]; [Gallop 2004, fig. 2][gallop04].

### Gaps the game must invent

- No verified matrix, inscription or impression for Sultan Mahmud’s Melaka port administration was located in the sources reviewed. A royal document stamp for 1511, a shahbandar’s clearance mark, and a device applied over a tied cargo closure are three separate design decisions. Do not derive any of them from the Ming silver seals or assign the same design to all three. [Ma Huan, pp. 108–109][ma]; [Pires, II, p. 265][pires]; [Gallop 2004, figs. 2, 11][gallop04].

**Artist visual notes:** Use the Aceh permit and Pahang treaty as references for how an impression reads against handwriting, not as assets to trace for Melaka. If the game needs an invented official impression, use a visibly distinct treatment from a brush mark, a stamp in metal, or a closure. Keep the Ming gifts off ordinary cargo. [Gallop 2004, figs. 2, 11][gallop04]; [Ma Huan, pp. 108–109][ma]; [Albuquerque, III, ch. XVIII][albu].

## 2. Merchant marks

### Documented fact

- **Near-period regional evidence — marks with unknown function.** In the Chinese brown-ware group from the late-fifteenth-century Santa Cruz wreck in the Philippines, some large jars have stamped or incised marks on the shoulder. The report does not identify the marks as merchants’ names or clearance stamps. Fig. 18 is a jar-shape reference; the text on p. 318 is the evidence for the shoulder marks. [Orillaneda, pp. 317–319, fig. 18][santa].
- **Earlier regional evidence — actual ink labels.** The Song-period Nanhai I wreck off China has Dehua-style ceramics with ink inscriptions on their bases, including surnames and stylized signs. Li illustrates a marked porcelain base and discusses repeated marks in relation to the distribution of objects on the ship. This is a comparator for a different kind of mark, centuries earlier, not a Melaka cargo label. [Li 2020, §2, fig. 6][li]; [Xu et al. 2024, “Archaeological background”][xu].
- **Western Indian Ocean comparison — allocations, not marks.** A Geniza memorandum written in India around 1110 assigns textile bales and portions of their contents to named people. It shows that the sender could specify interests in a shipment; it does not tell us what was marked on the outside of the bales. [T-S 8.27, recto lines 2–8][geniza-letter].

### Scholarly inference

- Li interprets some Nanhai I ink marks as ownership labels, using their concentration in a particular cargo area as supporting evidence. Xu and colleagues report competing readings for an individual sign (procurement or ownership versus a stylized signature). A repeated ink mark is thus a reason to look for a person or lot, not sufficient on its own to determine owner, production site, route, or legality. This is a defensible inspection principle, not a recovered Melaka rule. [Li 2020, §2][li]; [Xu et al. 2024, “Archaeological background” and “Results and discussion”][xu].

### Gaps the game must invent

- No verified dictionary of Melaka merchant marks, Gujarati bale marks, or standard national insignia emerged from these sources. Choose any recurring merchant- or partnership-specific marks as explicit fiction. A kiln mark, ornament, ownership label and official seal should not be interchangeable in the manual. [Orillaneda, pp. 305, 317–319][santa]; [Li 2020, §2][li]; [T-S 8.27, recto][geniza-letter]; [Blueprint, §§06–07][blueprint].

**Artist visual notes:** Put prototype brush marks on the *underside* of some small ceramics and model incised or impressed traces on some jar *shoulders*. Keep glaze decoration separate. If a bale has a painted sign or a tied tag, label its look and placement as invented; avoid giving all traders on one route the same mark. [Li 2020, fig. 6][li]; [Orillaneda, pp. 305, 317–319][santa]; [T-S 8.27, recto][geniza-letter]; [Blueprint, §07][blueprint].

## 3. Cargo documents

### Documented fact

- **Melaka around the transition.** Pires, writing in 1512–1515, says four shahbandars received junk captains according to jurisdiction, presented them to the bendahara, assigned warehouses and dispatched merchandise. Cortesão translates part of the passage as “provide them with lodging if they have documents”; the passage does not identify those documents or give their fields. Pires also refers to a register of the accounts he kept in the Portuguese factory. Barbosa reports that leading Melaka gold merchants reckoned their accounts in *bahares*. These attest different uses of records; none is a surviving pre-conquest ship manifest. [Pires, I, author’s address to the king; II, p. 265][pires]; [Barbosa, II, pp. 175–176][barbosa].
- **A dated cargo report from Portuguese Melaka.** In his letter of 6 January 1514, Captain Rui de Brito Patalim reports that three Borneo junks brought edible camphor, seed pearls and provisions and took Cambay and Keling cloth on the return. This is an official letter reporting trade, not paperwork submitted by the junks themselves. [Patalim, 6 Jan. 1514, fol. 2, *Historia Obscura* doc. 2][patalim].
- **Regional storage and maritime rules.** Ma Huan describes the Ming fleet’s enclosed warehouses and granaries at early-fifteenth-century Melaka, where it kept money and provisions. The transmitted *Undang-Undang Laut Melaka* distinguishes the master and *kiwi*, people with an interest in the voyage and part of its cargo. The surviving RAS Maxwell Malay 128 manuscript is a later copy; its catalogue records the maritime laws’ date as AH 1066 (1656) and catalogues the physical manuscript as possibly nineteenth-century. Neither the warehouse account nor the legal roles supplies a standard cargo form. [Ma Huan, “The Country of Man-la-chia”][ma]; [Sudarman et al., p. 84, nn. 74–75][uul-study]; [RAS, Maxwell Malay 128][ras].
- **Portuguese cartaz — navigation permission, not cargo declaration.** Scholarship places the cartaz in the early-sixteenth-century Portuguese Indian Ocean regime (Mueller and Ayello date its institution to 1502). It licensed passage; permission could entail restrictions on where a ship called and what it carried. The date makes it possible for a Portuguese pass to exist somewhere in the region in 1511, not for the sultanate to be its issuer or for a Portuguese requirement to apply to every ship entering pre-conquest Melaka. [Mueller and Ayello, “How the East Was Lost,” discussion of the cartaz][mueller]; [Prakash, “European and Asian Merchants,” discussion of the cartaz][prakash]; [Raman, introduction][raman].
- **Surviving visual comparisons.** The 1511 Cochin Portuguese factory inventory survives on paper (Torre do Tombo, Corpo Cronológico II, mç. 28, doc. 35, fol. 2r). Near-period regional handwriting also survives in an Arabic letter from Pasai dated by internal evidence to about 1516 and in the Malay Jawi letters from Ternate dated to 1521–1522. These are a factory inventory and diplomatic correspondence, not Melaka manifest specimens. [Paper in Motion, item 44, facsimile][cochin]; [Gallop 2015, fig. IV and n. 8][jawi]; [Blagden, pp. 87–101, opening extract][ternate].

### Scholarly inference

- Winstedt dates the maritime digest to Sultan Mahmud’s reign but documents conflicting attributions in manuscript copies; Manguin reads it chiefly as a set of rules aboard large trading ships. Use the digest for roles and disputes, not as a dated administrative manual. Pires’s unnamed documents, merchants’ accounts, and the distinction between master and cargo interests make a comparison of different records and testimony reasonable as gameplay; those sources do not establish one universal paperwork workflow. [Winstedt 1953, pp. 31–33][winstedt]; [Manguin 2022, para. 14][manguin]; [Pires, II, p. 265][pires]; [Barbosa, II, pp. 175–176][barbosa]; [Sudarman et al., p. 84][uul-study].

### Gaps the game must invent

- Treat the blueprint’s “manifest” as a player-readable **declaration** with vessel, captain, route, owners, lots, quantity, grade and value. Required fields, paper layout, filing procedure, and whether an individual entry comes from a shipmaster or a merchant are design choices. Do not prefill expected marks, knots, seals or packaging on the declaration; keep expectations in a separate manual and treat discrepancies as leads. If a Portuguese pass appears, put its authority and permission on a separate prop, not on the sultanate’s intake sheet. [Blueprint, §06][blueprint]; [Pires, II, p. 265][pires]; [Prakash, discussion of the cartaz][prakash]; [Raman, introduction][raman].

**Artist visual notes:** For a Portuguese hand, start with the Cochin folio; for regional script texture, use the Pasai and Ternate exemplars with the scripts correctly identified. For the actual inspection UI, use readable transcribed rows. Do not copy royal correspondence into a cargo form, or present the game’s layout as a facsimile. [Paper in Motion, item 44][cochin]; [Gallop 2015, fig. IV and n. 8][jawi]; [Blagden, opening extract][ternate]; [Blueprint, §06][blueprint].

## 4. Packaging: bales, jars, chests and sacks

### Documented fact

| Type | What the evidence actually shows |
| --- | --- |
| **Bales** | Barbosa names Cambay cloth in Melaka’s trade and describes victuals being packed, without specifying a bale’s shape, wrap or ties. The earlier India-to-Aden Geniza memorandum explicitly names textile bales, as well as bales of lac and spikenard; it is not an image or a Melaka record. [Barbosa, II, §110, pp. 174–176][barbosa]; [T-S 8.27, recto lines 2–8 and verso address][geniza-letter]. |
| **Jars** | Barbosa describes large, black-glazed jars at Martaban, some large enough in the translation to hold “a pipe of water.” At Santa Cruz, thin porcelain cups were found inside stoneware jars. The wreck report illustrates bulbous brown/olive jars with short necks and shoulder handles, and shows ceramics still in their packing positions in the lower hull. At the earlier Bakau wreck off Indonesia, Flecker illustrates Thai grey unglazed stoneware jars up to about 90 cm tall with everted rims and four lugs. Pepper-like material in some jars was not securely identified. [Barbosa, II, §106, pp. 158–159][barbosa]; [Orillaneda, pp. 305, 317–319, 322–323, figs. 18, 21][santa]; [Flecker, “The ceramic cargo,” figs. 6–7][bakau]. |
| **Chests** | An earlier Indian Ocean baggage list names *fātiya* containers, translated as “chests” by Lambourn, including a bamboo example containing locks and others for glassware. These are listed by a traveler in India, not excavated Melaka cargo chests. For physical wood-and-lid references, the *Mary Rose* carpenter’s second chest has wooden handles and had a hinged lid and a lock opening; that is English shipboard personal storage from 1545, not an Asian packing standard. [T-S NS 324.114, verso][geniza-inventory]; [Lambourn, introduction, lines 8–17][lambourn]; [Mary Rose Museum, “The Carpenter’s cabin and chests”][maryrose]. |
| **Sacks** | The same earlier baggage list names a large sack of rice, small sacks of rice for the voyage and rice carried in baskets. It gives more than one container type for a commodity; it does not provide the weave, a knot diagram, or evidence for an obligatory Melaka rice sack. [T-S NS 324.114, verso lines 1–7][geniza-inventory]; [Lambourn, introduction, lines 1–7][lambourn]. |

**Local archaeology and limits.** The Museum of Asian Art’s coastal Melaka exhibition includes Chinese and Southeast Asian storage jars and Burmese Martaban jars from reclamation-era discoveries; the assemblage spans periods and includes abundant Qing material. Do not date a displayed jar to 1511 from its association with those finds alone. The first two phases of the Pulau Melaka wreck investigation yielded ceramics and coins and the 2025 paper proposed a late-fifteenth- to early-sixteenth-century date by comparison. Later reporting from the third phase describes radiocarbon results suggesting a thirteenth-century age for sampled wood, alongside Sultanate-era coins and porcelain at the site. This is not a securely dated, closed 1511 cargo assemblage. The *Flor de la Mar* is known from the loss narrative, not a verified archaeological collection of Melaka cargo. [Museum of Asian Art, “Port and Pottery”][malacca-museum]; [Saiffuddin et al., abstract][pulau]; [Bernama, third-phase field reporting][bernama]; [Albuquerque, III, ch. XLII][albu]; [Tjoa-Bonatz, “Sea Routes in Sumatran Waters”][flor].

### Scholarly inference

- Orillaneda interprets cups found inside jars as packing. His excavation photographs support using the container and contents as independent inspection clues; they do not show every closure or establish what a Melaka jar had to contain. “Martaban” is also a broad trade or collecting name for storage jars with more than one production origin, not an automatic proof a jar was made in Martaban. [Orillaneda, pp. 305, 322–325][santa]; [Princessehof, “The Collection of Chinese and Southeast Asian Jars”][martaban].
- Cloth bales and rice sacks are reasonable designs by analogy, given Barbosa’s trade account and the earlier Indian Ocean descriptions. A plain chest for a smaller protected consignment is also plausible by analogy. None of those transfers establishes the actual local bundle dimensions, sack fiber, or joinery; the comparison sources are strongest on function, not on Melaka appearance. [Barbosa, II, pp. 174–176][barbosa]; [T-S 8.27, recto][geniza-letter]; [Lambourn, introduction, lines 1–17][lambourn].

### Gaps the game must invent

- Choose the bale wrap and lashings, sack fibers and seams, chest boards and fastenings, and jar lids, ties and cushioning as reconstructions. No source reviewed ties a specific knot or closure to a Melaka merchant community or documents a standard tamper seal around a storage-jar mouth. Keep alternate packing and repacking possible rather than encoding a package type as an automatic customs violation. [Orillaneda, pp. 305, 317–323][santa]; [Flecker, “The ceramic cargo”][bakau]; [T-S 8.27, recto][geniza-letter]; [Lambourn, introduction][lambourn]; [Blueprint, §06][blueprint].

**Artist visual notes:** Start with the Santa Cruz fig. 18 jar profile and figs. 3–4 for glazed tableware, then use fig. 21 to stage the hold. Use Bakau figs. 6–7 for another jar silhouette (the photo in fig. 6 shows jars moved by salvors, not original stowage). Show a sturdy stoneware shipping jar and finer porcelain contents as distinct materials even if the game groups them in one commodity UI. Contrast a tightly bound, blockier cloth pack, a bulging gathered sack, and a rigid lidded container for readability; those last three silhouettes and their fastenings are art direction, not local reconstructions from specimens. [Orillaneda, pp. 304–305, 318–319, 323, figs. 3–4, 18, 21][santa]; [Flecker, figs. 6–7][bakau]; [Lambourn, introduction][lambourn]; [Mary Rose Museum, “The Carpenter’s cabin and chests”][maryrose]; [Blueprint, §07][blueprint].

## Suggested Detailed Inspection case

**Design proposal, not a historical incident.** The declaration lists a lot of porcelain cups with a quantity and owner. Opening a stoneware jar exposes the cups, which is supported by Santa Cruz. A repeated brush mark underneath some cups (an older regional analogue) gives the player a lead. The manual associates it with a fictional trading partnership, not a country. A second lot or a merchant’s explanation can clarify why some cups carry a different mark; pursue the count, weight, contents and testimony rather than making the mark a guilt flag. Leave an official permit, if used, separate from the cargo declaration. [Orillaneda, p. 305][santa]; [Li 2020, §2][li]; [Raman, introduction][raman]; [Blueprint, §06 and “Example Play Scenario”][blueprint].
