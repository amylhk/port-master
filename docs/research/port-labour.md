# Who worked the port of Melaka around 1511?

Research for [gameplay blueprint v0.5, Stages C and D](../gameplay-blueprint.md#13-development-blueprint-from-vision-to-playable-game). Companion notes: [port offices and customs](port-official.md), [ships and weights](ships-weights-currency.md), [cargo records and packaging](seals-marks-packaging.md), [trade and seasonality](commodities.md), and [the conquest and its aftermath](1511-events.md).

- **[Documented fact]** means a source attests the practice, rule or reported event as described. A chronicler's estimate is evidence of what was reported, not a census; a legal prescription is not evidence of how often it was followed.
- **[Scholarly inference]** means a named scholar's reconstruction or an interpretation of the cited evidence, including the limits of what was found.
- **[Suggested gameplay abstraction]** means a design choice, not a historical claim.

## 1. Moving and keeping cargo

| Work | What the evidence supports | Numbers, pay and limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Porters and stevedores** | **[Documented fact]** Pires says merchants unloaded on arrival. In his account of Sequeira's 1509 visit, merchandise was put into godowns and goods were being weighed on land. Shahbandars gave orders for elephants. ([Pires], II, pp. 255, 257, 265, 273.) **[Scholarly inference]** Thomaz identifies cash-purchased enslaved people (*belati*) among the ruler's dockworkers, sailors and oarsmen, and loading and unloading among the occupations of enslaved people in Melaka more broadly. These accounts identify part of the workforce, not everyone who carried a load. ([Thomaz], p. 75; [Thomaz, “Town and Its Society”][thomaz-town], §II.) | **[Scholarly inference]** Ship's hands, merchant dependants and royal labour could all have participated at different handoffs. The passages do not give a gang size, regular porter wage, official porter title or evidence of a porter guild. |
| **Boatmen and lighters** | **[Documented fact]** Pires records small *paraos* in the Straits; in his account of 1509 the ruler refers to people who go out to sell chickens to the Portuguese at anchor. ([Pires], II, pp. 256–57, 264.) **[Scholarly inference]** Small river and coastal craft and an offshore anchorage make ship-to-shore lighterage (transferring cargo between the ship and land) plausible; the sources do not show that every large vessel used it. ([Meilink-Roelofsz], pp. 37, 43; [Thomaz, “Town and Its Society”][thomaz-town], §III.) | **[Scholarly inference]** No 1511 lighter fleet count, crew complement, tariff or monopoly was identified. *Perahu* is a broad boat term, not a certified lighter class. ([Liaw], p. 135, §29; [Manguin 2022], para. 57.) |
| **Orang Laut and royal rowing** | **[Documented fact]** Pires says the Bintan governor brought men he calls *Celates* to row when the king required them, for certain months of the year, “without payment, just for their food.” ([Pires], II, p. 264.) **[Scholarly inference]** Thomaz identifies *Celates* with Orang Laut and reads naval manpower as a form of service owed by maritime dependencies, while stressing the ruler's incomplete control over them. ([Thomaz], pp. 76–77.) | **[Scholarly inference]** This is a specific service obligation with subsistence, not a commercial lighterage wage, a count of boatmen, or proof that Orang Laut as a group handled Melaka's cargo transfers. |
| **Warehouse keepers** | **[Documented fact]** Pires assigns the allocation of warehouses to the four shahbandars; he separately says merchants were rebuilding godowns after the conquest. ([Pires], II, pp. 265, 283.) **[Scholarly inference]** Allocation, ownership and daily custody need not have belonged to the same person. Thomaz places higher-status royal dependants in merchandise management, but does not identify them as the keepers of every godown (warehouse; Malay *gudang*). ([Thomaz], p. 75; [Meilink-Roelofsz], pp. 40, 42–43.) | **[Scholarly inference]** Someone had to receive and release goods, but there is no keeper roster, staffing ratio or keeper's pay rate in these passages, nor a basis to put every merchant's store on a royal payroll. |

## 2. Measuring, recording and guarding

- **[Documented fact] Weighing, fee collection and standards.** Pires says the king had “secretaries and receivers” for the due on goods weighed entering or leaving: **one *calaim* on each hundred of value**. The land digest assigns rules for weights and measures to the shahbandar. Neither identifies who physically operated each scale. ([Pires], II, p. 274; [Liaw], p. 135, §29.)
- **[Documented fact] Assay is a separate job.** Pires reports an exclusive royal gold-assaying office: the holder gave the king **half a *cate* of gold annually**, charged the public **one *calaim* per *tael*** and did not charge the king and mandarins. These are an office payment and a service charge, not pay for an ordinary weigher. ([Pires], II, p. 276.)
- **[Documented fact] Valuers are not weighers.** For some big ships Pires describes a panel of **ten merchants (five Klings and five from another nation)** valuing cargo before the temenggung as Customs Judge. Pires also reports theft by guards and bribery of valuers as reasons for the arrangement. Ten is the size of this panel, not a permanent customs headcount. ([Pires], II, p. 273.)
- **[Documented fact] Clerks and scribes.** In its description of court procedure, the *Sejarah Melayu* places “all servants and clerks of the Raja,” the shahbandar and those in charge of state revenues under the Treasurer (*penghulu bendahari*). Pires names the royal secretaries and receivers in the narrower weighing context. ([Brown], p. 58; [Pires], II, p. 274.)
- **[Scholarly inference]** The Treasurer's responsibility for royal staff and the temenggung's authority at customs can coexist; neither supplies a line-by-line organisation chart. Keep procedures for handling cargo, valuing it, recording a due and receiving payment distinct without assuming one separate employee per step. The sources do not give a sultanate clerical establishment, a routine manifest template or a clerk's rate of pay. ([Brown], p. 58; [Pires], II, pp. 265, 273–74; [cargo records note](seals-marks-packaging.md#3-cargo-documents).)
- **[Documented fact] Guards.** Pires gives the city guard and first hearing of prison cases to the temenggung, who has “many people under his jurisdiction”; he distinguishes the laksamana's jurisdiction at sea and role as the king's guard. The *Sejarah Melayu* depicts Sultan Alauddin questioning his temenggung about a night watch after a merchant's chest was stolen. Neither gives a watch roster or guard wages. ([Pires], II, pp. 264–65; [Brown], pp. 113–15.)
- **[Documented fact] A guard detail from a different decade and employer.** Ma Huan describes the Ming treasure fleet's enclosed stores at early-fifteenth-century Melaka: watch-drums at **four gates** and nightly patrols with bells. This describes a fleet compound, not a 1511 sultanate warehouse guard. ([Ma Huan], “The Country of Man-la-chia,” linked excerpt p. 4.)
- **[Documented fact] Who answered to the four shahbandars and the temenggung?** Pires's **four** is the number of officeholders serving merchant constituencies (see [the constituencies](port-official.md#3-number-communities-served-appointment)). Their work included receiving captains and dispatching merchandise. The *Commentaries* calls the temenggung Customs Judge “over all these”; Pires says he received merchandise dues. The land digest §24.1 separately recognises enslaved people belonging to a shahbandar; it does not specify their duties. ([Pires], II, p. 265; [Commentaries], III, pp. 87–88; [Liaw], pp. 121–23, §24.1.)
- **[Scholarly inference]** Andaya reconstructs a city where senior officials' followers and retainers clustered around their patrons, including a temenggung quarter. This supports patron-based service alongside public office, not four identical port departments. Nothing in these sources gives each shahbandar a fixed complement of clerks, interpreters, warehouse hands or guards, or tells us how many of the temenggung's people worked at customs rather than on city duties. ([Andaya], pp. 90–91; [Pires], II, pp. 265, 273–74.)

## 3. Enslavement, debt service and compensation

- **[Scholarly inference] Status was not a job title.** Liaw distinguishes people held as ordinary slaves (*hamba* among several terms), debt bondspeople (*orang berhutang*), and the ruler's slaves (*hamba raja*). Thomaz distinguishes higher-status royal dependants entrusted with merchandise from cash-purchased *belati* doing harder manual work. Fujitani notes both the variety of status within royal service and the ambiguity of titles and deferential language. These are overlapping ways of describing status and work, not a single universal set of ranks. ([Liaw], pp. 40–41; [Thomaz], p. 75; [Fujitani], pp. 143–47.)
- **[Documented fact] Debt could compel service without making a debtor's family automatically servile.** In the transmitted land laws, §43.8 limits claims and requires acknowledgment and witnesses before claiming against a debtor's family in the case it describes; §44.1 says the husband enters service while his wife and children do not, even where they share responsibility for the debt. On his death in service it specifies **one third** of the debt payable by them in that case. These are provisions, not records of actual port contracts. ([Liaw], pp. 167–69, §§43.8, 44.1.)
- **[Documented fact] Labour could be hired or borrowed through a slaveholder.** The land digest distinguishes such arrangements and assigns liability in cases such as loss of goods, injury or death. These clauses address tasks such as selling on someone's behalf and climbing trees; they are not a dock hiring tariff. ([Liaw], pp. 89–91, §§15.1–15.2.)
- **[Scholarly inference]** Liaw reads repayment as a path out of bondage. Fujitani connects debt bondspeople with sailors, dockworkers and skilled trades in early Portuguese-era evidence, and reads a 1521 account looking back to the sultanate as evidence that some received service remuneration. His identification of those workers as debt bondspeople and of the pay as likely cash are interpretations; the account gives no 1511 rate. ([Liaw], p. 41; [Fujitani], pp. 145–48; [Reid], pp. 120–21, 131–34, for regional context.)

| Number or compensation | What it measures |
|---|---|
| **[Documented fact] 1,500** | The *Commentaries* reports **1,500 people formerly enslaved by the sultan**, brought with their women and children after the conquest. It says Albuquerque ordered wages and provisions for their work on the fortress “in accordance with the native custom,” and that when not called they worked for themselves. No wage rate is stated; this is a reported transfer and mobilisation, not the size of a pre-conquest dock crew. The same passage distinguishes other captives compelled to work in chains. ([Commentaries], III, p. 135.) |
| **[Scholarly inference] 1,600 or 3,000 royal; 3,500 privately held by merchants** | Thomaz cites divergent estimates for people enslaved by the ruler and a separate estimate for those held by merchants, in his discussion of the sultan's trading enterprise. His other survey gives roughly **2,000** formerly held by the sultanate out of **5,500** enslaved people in town at the transition. These are estimates with different scopes, not independent headcounts to add together and not counts of port occupations. ([Thomaz], p. 75 and n. 7; [Thomaz, “Town and Its Society”][thomaz-town], §II.) |
| **[Documented fact] About 5,000–6,000** | The *Commentaries* attributes this many enslaved Javanese people to Utimutiraja and his family in its account of the siege. It is a chronicler's claim about a powerful merchant household group, not dock staffing or a figure to reconcile arithmetically with Thomaz's estimates. ([Commentaries], III, p. 109; compare [1511 events](1511-events.md).) |
| **[Scholarly inference] Cargo allowances, not dock wages** | Meilink-Roelofsz reads the transmitted maritime code as allowing free and enslaved seamen **two and one *koyan*** respectively on large ships, and **600 and 300 *gantang*** on smaller ships as shares in cargo. This describes shipboard trade participation in the code, not daily pay for shore workers or lighters; see Manguin on the code's shipboard scope. ([Meilink-Roelofsz], pp. 46–48; [Manguin 2022], para. 20.) |
| **[Documented fact] 30 November 1513: half or one *ganta* of rice per person** | In the Portuguese ration account transcribed and translated by Fujitani, four carpenters and four blacksmiths serving at the fortress and docks received **one *ganta* each**; **225 others**, including **99** from the jailhouse, received **half a *ganta* each**. This is one dated distribution for a combined construction workforce, not a total for the port or a sultanate wage schedule. ([Fujitani], pp. 149–50 and n. 43, citing Francisco Cardoso, [Torre do Tombo CC/2/53/93](https://digitarq.arquivos.pt/details?id=3793515).) |
| **[Documented fact] 1519: 24.5 or 17.5 *gantas* per month** | Fujitani's table of the later Portuguese sea-service rates lists **24.5** for scribes and pilots, **17.5** for interpreters. These figures attest differentiated compensation under the Portuguese, not pay for shahbandar staff in 1511. ([Fujitani], p. 153, table 1, column B.) |

**[Scholarly inference]** The evidence supports several forms of compensation or obligation, not one pay scale: provisioning for required royal rowing, revenues attached to a specialist office, shipboard cargo shares, and later Portuguese rice payments. None can be converted into an ordinary 1511 porter's hourly rate. The sources checked do not support a total port headcount or numerical ratios for the civilian workforce.

## 4. Gameplay implications

- **[Suggested gameplay abstraction] Stage C — staff the handoffs, not the entire city.** Model boat transfer where needed, unloading, scales and records, and warehouse intake as separate capacity limits. Let the player request crews via the relevant shahbandar, merchant or captain; allow the same people to cover multiple jobs. Use crew slots or service costs tuned for the game, never present them as historical wage data. A shipment should be able to clear inspection but still wait for transport or storage.
- **[Suggested gameplay abstraction] Stage C — make delegation legible.** Give a clerk and scale operator different jobs in the UI, even if represented by one team. A merchant panel can resolve an exceptional valuation while the temenggung's customs staff remain responsible for collection and enforcement. Show which handoff is holding up a lot so the player can fix delays without manually handling every shipment.
- **[Suggested gameplay abstraction] Stage D — give staff patrons and competing obligations.** A shahbandar can broker merchant cooperation, the temenggung can redirect a city watch, and a royal rowing call can draw away people or boats a character expected to use. These are scenario mechanics, not evidence that the same workers held all three posts. Use the blueprint's proposed guilds only as fiction unless a specific institution is independently documented.
- **[Suggested gameplay abstraction] Stage D — portray coercion through people and consequences.** Distinguish hired crews, dependants, enslaved people and debt bondspeople in character stories. Give people relationships and goals, and make family separation, requisition or disputed repayment matter without turning ownership of people into a staffing upgrade. If the campaign crosses the conquest, treat Portuguese rationing and reassignment as a regime change rather than a default system for the sultanate.
