# Melaka c. 1509–1511: Commodity Prices and How They Moved

Research for Stage C ("Living Economy") and Stage D ("Narrative and Political Layer") of `docs/gameplay-blueprint.md`. This file covers **prices only**: what the 13 blueprint commodities cost at Melaka relative to one another, and how prices moved with the monsoons, with glut and scarcity, and with the 1511 conquest. It builds on, and does not repeat:

- `commodities.md`: origins, routes, grades and measures of each commodity.
- `ships-weights-currency.md`: bahar weights, coinage, ship values.
- `port-official.md`: customs rates, presents, the collective bazaar valuation.
- `1511-events.md`: the political narrative of 1509–1511.

**Tags.** Every claim carries one tag:

- `[Documented fact]`: stated in a primary source (page cited).
- `[Scholarly inference]`: derived from sources by arithmetic or argued by a historian. A derived number is never a quotation.
- `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]`: a design proposal, not history.

---

## 1. The evidence problem

- `[Documented fact]` No Melaka price list from 1509–1511 survives. The closest source is Tomé Pires, *Suma Oriental*, written in Melaka and India in 1512–1515. Pires gives Melaka prices for only a few goods: cloves, white sandalwood, calambac and camphor. For other goods he gives prices at their source ports (mace in Banda, cinnamon in Ceylon, lac in Martaban) or the value of whole ship cargoes (Pires 1944, I: 92–214; II: 269–285).
- `[Documented fact]` Prices in Melaka were set collectively, not item by item. Ten to twenty merchants valued a cargo together, the price was fixed and the goods divided among them in proportion. Pires says "the law and the prices of merchandise in Malacca are well known" (Pires 1944, II: 273–274). The mechanism is described in `port-official.md`.
- `[Scholarly inference]` In practice, a "Melaka price" was a negotiated cargo valuation, usually paid in barter goods (cloth for spices, pepper for silk), rather than a posted retail price. Meilink-Roelofsz (1962) draws the same picture of the Asian trade from Pires: a pedlar-and-commission trade run through brokers and partnerships.
- `[Scholarly inference]` So the figures below fall into three grades: (a) a Melaka unit price stated by Pires; (b) a source-port price or a ratio between goods; (c) a relative rank argued from route, duty and measure. Each row in §3 says which grade it is.

## 2. Converting to a common unit

The common unit here is the **Portuguese cruzado (cz)**, the money Pires uses for valuations.

| Unit | Value in cruzados | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 100 tin cash (*caixas*) | 1 calaim = 0.03 cz | Pires 1944, II: 275 `[Documented fact]` |
| 100 calains | 3 cz (so 1 cz = 33⅓ calains) | Pires 1944, II: 275 `[Documented fact]` |
| 1 calaim | 11 reis 4 ceitis (cruzado ≈ 400 reis in Pires' reckoning) | Pires 1944, I: 99, 169 n. `[Documented fact]` |
| 1 tael of 8-mate gold | 256 calains ≈ 7.7 cz | Pires 1944, II: 275 `[Documented fact]` |
| 1 tael of Pegu silver | 100 calains per 3 taels ≈ 1 cz | Pires 1944, II: 276 `[Documented fact]` |
| 1 tael of Siam/China silver | "formerly 40 calains", i.e. 1.2 cz, "now somewhat more" | Pires 1944, II: 276 `[Documented fact]` |
| 1 cz in tin bullion | ≈ 69 lb (≈ 31.3 kg) of tin | Cortesão's note in Pires 1944, II: 270 n. `[Scholarly inference]` |

- `[Documented fact]` Weights came in two bahars. The small bahar (about 200 cates) was used for silk, musk, opium, quicksilver, **copper** and vermilion. The large bahar (about 205 cates) was used for pepper, cloves, **nutmeg**, mace, benzoin, lac, **incense**, brazilwood and alum (Pires 1944, II: 277–278; Nunes 1554, in Felner 1868).
- `[Scholarly inference]` This file uses the metric values already adopted in `ships-weights-currency.md`: **small bahar ≈ 183.6 kg** and **large bahar ≈ 210.3 kg**. Prices "per kg" below are derived figures given only for comparison.
- `[Documented fact]` Rice was sold by volume, by the *gantang*. A Melaka *gamta* of rice weighed 3 arráteis 10 oz by the new measure, about 1.66 kg (Pires 1944, II: 277–278). The Javanese ganta was smaller: 25 Java gantas made 20 Melaka gantas (Pires 1944, I: 178).
- `[Scholarly inference]` Ship-cargo values (Gujarat 15,000–30,000 cz a ship, Cambay up to 70,000–80,000 cz; see `ships-weights-currency.md`) cannot be split into unit prices, because Pires never says how much of each good a ship carried. They are used here only to rank how much capital a route moved.
- `[Documented fact]` One more benchmark: the Bendahara gave the Portuguese captives of 1509–1511 Cambay cloth worth 10,000 calains to trade with, about **300 cz** (Albuquerque 1880, III; context in `1511-events.md`).

- `[Documented fact]` Some luxury goods give a ceiling for Melaka prices. Calambac (eaglewood): 2 arráteis "fetch six or seven cruzados, and there is some worth twelve" (Pires I: 112). Borneo camphor: 12–40 cz a catty (Pires I: 132).
- `[Scholarly inference]` Per kilogram, calambac and camphor were roughly 100–1,000 times dearer than cloves, and cloves roughly 6–8 times dearer than white sandalwood. The 13 blueprint goods therefore span about one order of magnitude per bahar: sandalwood and nutmeg around 1–2 cz, tin around 6 cz, spices around 9–12 cz. They do not reach the luxury tier.
- `[Scholarly inference]` **Corrections to `commodities.md`.** (1) The "9–10 cz plentiful / 12 cz scarce" quotation (Pires I: 214) is about **cloves**, not mace. Mace reaches that level only through Pires' Banda equivalence of 1 bahar mace = 1 bahar cloves (I: 207). The nutmeg figure derived from it is therefore an inference twice removed. (2) The Sunda figure "8 mates = 300 calains = 9 cz" (Pires I: 169) is the value of a **tumdaya of gold**, not of a bahar of pepper. Pires gives no Sunda pepper price. This file does not edit `commodities.md`; a later pass should fix it.

## 4. How prices moved

### 4.1 Monsoon season

- `[Documented fact]` Pires calls Melaka a place at "the end of monsoons and the beginning of others". Gujarati ships sailed from India in March. Coromandel (Kling) traders left Melaka in January and returned around October. Java voyages left in January and came back between May and August or September (Pires 1944, II: 270–272, 284).
- `[Documented fact]` On the Java run, a merchant who took the risk of 100 cz of goods received 140 cz, paid 44 days after the junk came home. Sunda, Siam and Pegu voyages returned about 50%. Bengal and Pulicat returned one for one or 80–90%, sometimes three for one. China sometimes returned three for one (Pires 1944, II: 284).
- `[Documented fact]` Pires says merchants preferred goods to gold "because from day to day the merchandise goes up in price" (Pires 1944, II: 285).
- `[Scholarly inference]` Prices followed the fleets. Western goods (cloth, glass, frankincense, copper, iron) were dearest just before the Gujarati and Kling fleets arrived and cheapest just after. Eastern goods (spices, sandalwood) were dearest just before the Java and Banda junks returned and cheapest when they unloaded. The voyage returns above are the largest spreads the sources record between buying and selling.

### 4.2 Glut and scarcity

- `[Documented fact]` Cloves: 9–10 cz in plenty, 12 cz in scarcity, a premium of about 20–33% (Pires I: 214). Martaban lac: 4 to 7 viças a bahar, i.e. 1.2–2.1 cz (I: 100). Martaban cowries: 15,000 to the viça usually, 16,000 when cheap, 14,000 when "very dear", a swing of about ±7% (I: 99).
- `[Documented fact]` Nutmeg was sold only together with mace, seven bahars to one, "because otherwise the merchandise could not stand it … the nutmeg would be lost" (Pires I: 207). Barbosa says mace and nutmeg were so plentiful in Banda that they were burned "and may be had almost for the asking" (Barbosa 1921, II: 197).
- `[Scholarly inference]` Nutmeg was in permanent glut at its source. Its price was supported by being bundled with mace, not by scarcity, so its price moves only gently.
- `[Documented fact]` Source-to-Melaka markups: mace 3–4 cz in Banda against about 9–12 cz in Melaka (I: 207–208, 214). Moluccan cloves: goods bought in Melaka for 500 reis (about 1¼ cz) bought a bahar in the Moluccas (I: 214).
- `[Scholarly inference]` The clove markup is therefore about 7–10 times. Spices and sandalwood had the steepest gradients from source to Melaka of any good in the blueprint.
- `[Scholarly inference]` Reid (1988–1993) argues that Melaka, like other Southeast Asian trading cities, depended on imported rice from Java, Siam and Pegu. A delay to the rice fleets was therefore the city's most direct scarcity risk. Pires' duty-free treatment of provisions (presents only) reflects that dependence.

### 4.3 The 1511 conquest

- `[Documented fact]` After the Portuguese took Melaka, the Javanese merchant leader Utemutaraja "intercepted all the cargoes of rice that had arrived, and would not suffer a single merchant to purchase any". He "bought up all the rice in the markets, which formed the staple article of food", and "there was a great scarcity of provisions" (Albuquerque 1880, III: chs. 33–34, pp. 144–148). The political side of this episode is in `1511-events.md`.
- `[Scholarly inference]` This is the only price shock of 1511 that is directly documented. It was a cornering of the rice market, not a lack of supply. The usual Javanese role (supplying rice) became the city's main weak point.
- `[Scholarly inference]` Meilink-Roelofsz (1962) and Thomaz (1993) argue that many Muslim merchants, especially Gujaratis, moved their business to other ports after the conquest (Pidie and Pasai, later Aceh, Johor/Bintan and Banten), while Kling, Chinese and some Javanese merchants stayed or came back. Pires' wording "used to come" for the Siamese rice junks (I: 107) and for the Malacca and Grisee junks to Banda and the Moluccas (I: 214) supports this.
- `[Scholarly inference]` The likely effect, which no source quantifies: Gujarati cloth and western goods grew scarcer and dearer in Melaka; eastern spices became easier to buy at Melaka than to ship west; and rice and provisions became volatile.

## 5. Gameplay abstractions

- `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` Use the cruzado as the hidden accounting unit and calains as the displayed small currency (1 cz = 33⅓ calains). The player sees cargoes valued the way Pires values them.
- `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` Base value index, with tin = 100 per large bahar:

| Good | Index | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Rice | 5 | Volume staple |
| Timber | 8 | Bulk |
| Sandalwood | 25 | 1.5 cz / bahar |
| Nutmeg | 25 | About mace ÷ 7 |
| Iron | 40 | Inferred |
| Porcelain | 120 | Inferred |
| Glassware | 120 | Inferred |
| Copper | 130 | Small-bahar good |
| Pepper | 140 | Inferred |
| Cloth | 150 | Inferred |
| Frankincense | 150 | Inferred |
| Silk | 220 | Inferred |

  Only tin, sandalwood and nutmeg are anchored in source figures. The other values are inferred from the ranks in §3.
- `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` Each good gets an arrival season, from §4.1. Its price falls 15–30% in the weeks after its fleet arrives and recovers as stock sells. Use the clove spread (about +25% in scarcity) as the default swing, with smaller swings for currency-like goods (tin ±5–7%, like the cowrie spread).
- `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` Bundling rule: nutmeg sells at full value only when the player holds mace (or high-grade spice) to sell with it. Unbundled nutmeg loses value, matching "the nutmeg would be lost" (Pires I: 207).
- `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` A post-conquest "rice cornering" event: rice price ×3–5 until the player breaks it, by escorting a Siam or Java rice fleet or by acting against the hoarder. Alongside it, cloth and western goods +20–40% and spices −10–20%, standing for the Gujarati departure.
- `[Suggested gameplay abstraction]` Model the collective valuation as one offer for a whole cargo (see `port-official.md`), not a price per item. Margins are then voyage-level percentages: 40% from Java, 50% from Siam or Pegu, up to 3× from China or Bengal.
